## What Lapsang Souchong is {#definition}

Zhengshan Xiaozhong is the red tea of Tongmu, in the Wuyi mountains of northern Fujian; its home villages and gardens lie inside the Wuyi mountains nature reserve. Zhengshan, true mountain, names the Tongmu core, and waishan, outer mountain, is tea made the same way outside it; how much that line says about quality is argued over. Lapsang Souchong is the Western name for it, taken from the Fuzhou dialect. In English the name now usually means the strongly smoked tea sold abroad, while in China Zhengshan Xiaozhong covers gentler and unsmoked tea too.

## Smoked and unsmoked {#smoke}

The traditional tea is dried over a fire of local pine, and the pine smoke gives it its main aroma, with longan fruit said to sit in the same note. The work is done in a qinglou, a wooden house of several floors with the fire at the bottom and the leaf on bamboo mats above, so the heat and smoke rise through the leaf. The strongly smoked style was made for export and never took hold in China. An unsmoked Tongmu red tea now sits beside it, built up from about 2005 and made widely for the Chinese market. Cheap Lapsang from outside Tongmu is often flavoured or smoked by a shortcut rather than made in a qinglou.

## How Lapsang is made {#making}

The leaf is withered, rolled and left to oxidise in covered baskets or cloth bags for some hours, until it turns reddish-brown to copper. It then goes into a hot pan, guo hong guo, which stops the oxidation fast, lifts the aroma and gives a sweeter, rounder cup, and is rolled again while hot, which shapes the strip and helps it take up smoke. In the traditional way the leaf is withered with pine heat in the qinglou and dried over the pine fire for most of a day.

## How Lapsang began {#history}

Zhengshan Xiaozhong is called the oldest red tea, the ancestor of red tea, born in Tongmu; the dates given for it differ. The usual story is of soldiers camped among the picked leaf in the harvest season: the leaf spoiled, the villagers dried it over pine fire, and the smoky tea was sold to Dutch buyers. The story is told, not recorded. The tea reached Europe first through Dutch and then British trade, under names such as Bohea and Souchong. In 2005 Tongmu makers devised Jin Jun Mei, a red tea of buds alone made the Lapsang way but not smoked, and it changed the village’s trade.

## Keeping and ageing Lapsang {#ageing}

Lapsang has no recognised tradition of long ageing, and no category of decades-old red tea is described. Over its first few years in good storage the pine smoke softens and moves toward dried fruit, wood and sweetness. Keep it sealed, dry, cool and away from light and other smells; a smoky tea scents whatever is near it.

## Brewing Lapsang {#brewing}

In a cup or pot, use about 3 grams for 240 ml, with water at 90 to 100°C, and steep about two to five minutes, usually around three. Gongfu style, use about 5 grams in a 100 to 110 ml gaiwan or pot, with short steeps that grow a little longer each round. Too hot or too long and the smoke turns harsh and bitter and covers the tea.

## What people often notice {#common-impressions}

In the smoked tea: pine and pine resin, longan or dried longan, campfire or woodsmoke, dried fruit and a sweet aftertaste. In the unsmoked tea: honey, longan and fruit or flowers.

## What people often notice in Lapsang kept for years {#notice-aged}

The smoke softer and further back, with dried fruit coming forward.
