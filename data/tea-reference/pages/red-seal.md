## What Red Seal means {#definition}

Red Seal is a name from the early state era of raw pu’er, tied to the cakes the state tea company sold in the 1950s under its Zhong Cha mark. It is better read as a famous old label than as a grade of leaf. Cakes from that period are now rare, and the name carries a great deal of weight with collectors.

## Where the name comes from {#original-era}

The original Red Seal cakes belong to what the trade often calls the masterpiece era, the 1950s and 1960s, when pu’er was made in state factories and sold by the China National Tea Corporation. The company’s Zhong Cha trademark was registered in 1951, and its wrappers carried the ring of red characters that gives the mark its look. Red Seal sits beside Blue Seal and Yellow Seal as one of the colour-named cakes of that time. Which factory made which cake, and the exact years, are argued over even among specialists, so this page does not name them.

## Most Red Seal today is not the original {#name-today}

The name outlived the cakes it first described. Zhong Cha wrappers and layouts were reused for decades, and the company licensed its brand to other makers in the late 1990s and early 2000s, a period when counterfeit and poor Zhong Cha tea was widely reported. Cakes called Red Seal or Red Mark are still made and sold today, including new pressings from modern factories. These are tea sold under the old name. They are not the 1950s cakes, and the name alone does not tell you their age, leaf or maker.

## Reading the name on a tea {#reading-the-name}

A wrapper that says Red Seal does not date the tea, because the Zhong Cha wrapper often carries no year. Real age and origin come from a documented chain of storage and sale, not from the words on the label. A tea sold today as Red Seal should be taken as using the name unless its seller can show it is a 1950s cake, and that kind of proof is rare and costly. The name says nothing, by itself, about where the leaf grew.
