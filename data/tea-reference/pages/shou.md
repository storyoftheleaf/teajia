## What ripe pu’er is {#definition}

Ripe pu’er is pu’er whose leaf is fermented in a warm, damp pile before it is pressed or sold loose. It is ready to drink soon after it is made, while raw pu’er skips the pile and is usually aged for years.

## Wet piling {#wet-piling}

Sun-dried leaf is wetted and heaped so that heat, moisture and microbes can work on it. The pile is turned as it warms and kept below about 65°C, and the process runs for about one to two months before the leaf is dried. Yunnan’s state factories brought the method into production in 1973, after a joint team studied faster piling in Guangdong.

## Young ripe pu’er {#age-young}

In its first few years a ripe pu’er often still carries the smell of the pile, a damp, earthy note the trade calls pile taste. It fades with airing and with time, and a rinse or two before drinking helps.

## Five to ten years {#age-settling}

Between about five and ten years the pile note has usually gone. The cup turns cleaner and rounder, and its sweetness comes forward.

## Ten years and more {#age-aged}

After ten years or more, a well-kept ripe pu’er grows smooth and deep, with woody, date-like or medicinal notes the trade calls aged aroma. Storage matters as much as age: dry, clean storage keeps it bright, and damp storage leaves it musty.

## Brewing ripe pu’er {#brewing}

Ripe pu’er takes boiling water and forgives a heavy hand. A common start is about 7 grams to 100 ml, one quick rinse, then short steeps that lengthen as the leaf opens; it gives eight or more good cups. From a cake, brick or tuo, ease a piece off along the edge with a pick rather than snapping it.

## What people often notice {#common-impressions}

People often describe ripe pu’er as smooth and sweet, with earthy or woody notes. A young ripe tea can carry a faint smell of the pile, which fades with airing or in the first steeps.
