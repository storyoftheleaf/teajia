## What ripe pu’er is {#definition}

Ripe pu’er is pu’er whose leaf is fermented in a warm, damp pile before it is pressed or sold loose. The pile was developed to give, in weeks, the dark and mellow cup that Hong Kong and Guangdong drinkers knew from raw pu’er aged for years. So ripe pu’er is ready to drink soon after it is made, while raw pu’er skips the pile and is usually aged for years.

## Wet piling {#wet-piling}

Sun-dried leaf is wetted and heaped so that heat, moisture and microbes can work on it. The heat comes from the microbes themselves, so the pile is covered to hold it in, then turned about once a week to spread it and let air in. It is kept below about 65°C, because hotter than that the leaf starts to char. After one to two months the leaf is dried. Yunnan’s state factories brought the method into production in 1973, after a joint team studied faster piling in Guangdong.

## How piling came to be {#piling-history}

Before 1949, pu’er already reached Hong Kong and Guangzhou red in the cup. Leaf was packed damp into bamboo baskets, the caravan journey could take a year, and merchants kept the tea for years more before selling it. When faster transport brought fresher tea in the 1950s, those drinkers missed the old taste, and the trade set out to hurry it. In Macau and Hong Kong, Lu Zhuxun sprinkled leaf with water to speed its change. From 1959 the Guangdong tea company in Guangzhou developed a faster fermentation, often blending Yunnan leaf with Guangdong leaf. Yunnan had its own beginnings too: dampening leaf for its pressed jin cha, and in 1958 a steam method at Xiaguan that cut fermentation to about fifteen days.

## Yunnan learns the pile {#piling-1973}

In 1973 a team of seven from the Kunming, Menghai and Xiaguan factories went to the Guangzhou tea factory to learn its wet piling. Guangzhou piled with warm water. In Kunming’s cooler, drier air that failed, and Kunming succeeded in 1974 with cold water, sending about ten tonnes to Hong Kong that year. Menghai and Xiaguan each worked out their own ways, and by 1975 Menghai’s method had settled into the ripe pu’er made today.

## Piling today {#piling-today}

Most traditional factories still pile on cement floors or in tiled pits, seasoned over years of use; a new floor is first broken in with wetted tea dust until it darkens and stops smelling. Some newer makers pile off the floor on perforated steel to keep the leaf cleaner. A pile can be a hundred kilograms or twenty tonnes, and each maker decides how far to take it: a lighter fermentation keeps some of the raw leaf’s liveliness, a heavier one gives a thicker, darker cup, and taken too far the leaf turns hard and charred.

## Young ripe pu’er {#age-young}

In its first few years a ripe pu’er often still carries the smell of the pile, a damp, earthy note left over from fermentation that the trade calls pile taste. Air carries it off, so it fades with airing and with time, and a rinse or two before drinking helps.

## Five to ten years {#age-settling}

Between about five and ten years the pile note has usually aired away. With it gone, the cup turns cleaner and rounder, and its sweetness comes forward.

## Ten years and more {#age-aged}

After ten years or more, a well-kept ripe pu’er grows smooth and deep, with woody, date-like or medicinal notes the trade calls aged aroma. The leaf keeps changing slowly with the air and moisture around it, which is why storage matters as much as age: dry, clean storage keeps it bright, and damp storage leaves it musty.

## Brewing ripe pu’er {#brewing}

Ripe pu’er takes boiling water: fermented, tightly packed leaf needs full heat to open, and it does not scald the way green tea can. A common start is about 7 grams to 100 ml and one quick rinse, which wakes the leaf and carries off dust and any pile smell. Then short steeps that lengthen as the leaf opens, so the cup stays clear rather than heavy; it gives eight or more good cups.

## Breaking a pressed tea {#brewing-pressed}

From a cake, brick or tuo, ease a piece off along the edge with a pick rather than snapping it. Whole leaves give up their flavour steadily; broken ones release it all at once and cloud the cup.

## What people often notice {#common-impressions}

A smooth, sweet cup with earthy or woody notes. A young ripe tea can carry a faint smell of the pile, which fades with airing or in the first steeps.

## What people often notice in aged ripe pu’er {#notice-aged}

A smooth, deep cup with woody, date-like or medicinal notes, which the trade calls aged aroma.
