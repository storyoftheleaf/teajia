## A regional grouping {#regional-grouping}

Xishuangbanna is the home of pu'er's most prized old-tree tea, and the trade sorts it by two groups of mountains on either side of the Lancang, as the Mekong is called in China. The old six, Youle, Gedeng, Yibang, Mangzhi, Manzhuan and Yiwu, lie mostly east of the river and were the famous tea mountains of the Qing period. The newer mountains, such as Nannuo, Bulang, Bada and Hekai, lie west of it. Buyers care because the mountain is the first thing a good raw pu'er is judged by: Yiwu is known for a soft cup with a long sweet finish, Bulang for a stronger, bitter-then-sweet one. Sources disagree on compass words for the river, but they agree the two groups sit on opposite banks.

## Mountain names {#mountain-names}

One contemporary grouping names twelve old tea mountains: Youle, Gedeng, Yibang, Mangzhi, Manzhuan, Yiwu, Xiao Mengsong, Nannuo, Hekai, Mengsong, Bulang, and Bada. [cite:ctma-xishuangbanna-twelve-mountains-2025] The first six are the old six; the rest are the newer mountains, and the "new six" is not fixed. Sources list between six and eight names for it, so expect the list to change from seller to seller. Youle is also called Jinuo Mountain, and Yiwu is also called Mansa. Some of these names are single mountains and others are wider tea areas, so a label that names one should be read with that in mind. They matter to buyers because a mountain name is how raw pu'er is priced and sold, and each carries its own reputation.

## Living tea culture {#living-culture}

The people who live on these mountains made the tea, and their choices shaped what buyers can find today. The Bulang are among the earliest tea growers here, and Bulang Mountain is named for them. The Hani keep old trees around Nannuo, Hekai and Pasha, and the Dai also grew tea. In the lowlands, Dai families mostly replaced old trees with higher-yield terrace tea from the 1950s to the 1980s. Mountain communities, many of them Bulang, mostly left their old gardens standing and opened new fields elsewhere. That is why old-tree pu'er survives, and why families who kept their trees gained so much when ancient-tree tea began to command high prices after 2007. Buyers who ask who grew a tea and from what trees are asking the right question.
