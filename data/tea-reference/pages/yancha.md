## What Wuyi rock tea is {#definition}

Wuyi rock tea, yan cha, is the oolong of the Wuyi mountains around Wuyishan City in northern Fujian, and only tea from that area may be sold under the name. The bushes grow among rock, in crevices, on ledges and on terraces in cliff country, in soil weathered from the rock, with much fog and rain. The leaf is twisted into long thin strips, not rolled into balls, and it is finished with a long charcoal roast.

## Rock rhyme {#rock-rhyme}

The quality the trade prizes in Wuyi tea is called rock rhyme, yan yun. It is described as something felt in the mouth as much as tasted: aroma, taste and body together, with a lasting sweet, mouth-watering aftertaste, and it is tied to the rocky ground the tea grows in. The Chinese phrase paired with it is yan gu hua xiang, rock bone and floral fragrance.

## Where in Wuyi it grows {#growing-areas}

The trade sorts Wuyi tea by where it grew. Zheng yan, true rock, is the core rocky area and the most prized, its best-known part called the three pits and two streams. Ban yan, half rock, is the ring of edges and foothills around it, whose tea has some rock rhyme but less, and is often the better value. Zhou cha is tea from the river banks and flat land, not from rock.

## How Wuyi rock tea is made {#making}

Wuyi tea follows the oolong steps: it is withered in the sun and then cooled indoors, shaken and rested, fixed in a hot pan or drum, rolled into strips and given a first drying. Much of it is now made with machines guided by hand judgement, and fully hand-made tea is rare. What sets it apart is the roast: over charcoal, in several rounds with rests of weeks or months between them, not in one pass. The roast dries the tea so it keeps, and it takes away the raw, grassy taste, deepens the tea and adds fruit and a charcoal fragrance. The roast is matched to the leaf: thick, mature leaf takes a stronger roast, thin and aromatic leaf a lighter one.

## Light, medium and full roast {#roast-levels}

Wuyi tea is labelled by its roast. Light roast keeps bright floral notes and is for drinking early. Medium roast is the balanced middle, floral and fruity together. Full roast shows the clearest charcoal and fire notes and keeps the longest. A freshly roasted tea is rested so the fire note settles, and the heavier the roast, the longer the rest.

## Da Hong Pao {#da-hong-pao}

Da Hong Pao, Big Red Robe, began with six old bushes on a cliff at Jiulongke in Wuyi, the mother trees, and picking from them stopped in 2006. Three kinds of tea are sold under the name: tea from the mother trees, which is extremely scarce, so any such claim deserves doubt; purebred tea from Qidan, the cultivar grown from cuttings of the mother trees; and commercial Da Hong Pao, which is most of what is sold and is usually a blend of several Wuyi cultivars. The name is also used loosely for other Wuyi teas.

## Rougui {#rougui}

Rougui, cassia, is a Wuyi cultivar named for the cinnamon-bark smell of its finished tea. It dates from the Qing dynasty and is now one of the most widely planted and best-selling Wuyi teas.

## Shui Xian {#shui-xian}

Shui Xian is the most widely grown and drunk Wuyi cultivar and usually the least expensive of the main teas. It came to Wuyi in the Qing dynasty from elsewhere in northern Fujian, and Wuyi Shui Xian is traditionally roasted a shade darker than most rock tea. Lao cong, old-bush Shui Xian, comes from older bushes, costs more and is said to carry a woody, mossy character of its own, and Shui Xian, old bush above all, is the Wuyi tea most often said to age well.

## Meizhan {#meizhan}

Meizhan is a Fujian cultivar that came from Anxi, and it is also made as Wuyi rock tea. It is less famous than Rougui and Shui Xian and is not one of the four famous bushes of Wuyi.

## How Wuyi rock tea began {#history}

Northern Fujian made tribute tea for the court in the Song dynasty. Early in the Ming dynasty the emperor ended the tribute of pressed tea cakes in favour of loose leaf, and tea making in the region turned to loose leaf. Oolong, partly oxidised tea, took shape in the Wuyi area between the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, around the end of the Ming and the start of the Qing.

## Keeping and ageing Wuyi rock tea {#ageing}

Well-made medium to full roast Wuyi tea is the kind kept for years; light roast is for early drinking and is the most likely to go green again, a raw, sour or muddy taste that comes from damp or under-dried tea and is the main fault of stored rock tea. The heavier the roast, the longer the tea is said to stay good. With age the sharp roast softens, the liquor grows thicker and smoother and the high aroma fades. A tea that has gone green can be roasted again at low heat to drive out the moisture, a skilled job that costs some quality each time. Keep it sealed, dry, cool and away from strong smells, and look at it now and then.

## Brewing Wuyi rock tea {#brewing}

Wuyi tea is usually brewed gongfu style with water at or near the boil, about 95 to 100°C, and about 5 to 8 grams of leaf in a 100 to 120 ml gaiwan, a third to a half full. A quick rinse, poured off at once, is common. Steeps start short, about 5 to 20 seconds, and grow a few seconds longer each round, and good rock tea gives seven steeps or more. In a larger pot, use about 2 to 5 grams for 250 to 350 ml and steep two to three minutes.

## What people often notice {#common-impressions}

Roast and stone fruit, a mineral, rocky texture and a long, sweet, mouth-watering finish. Light roast is bright and floral, medium roast fruity and rounded, full roast charcoal, wood and roasted nuts. Da Hong Pao is often floral, Rougui spicy with cinnamon, young Shui Xian sweet and smooth, and old-bush Shui Xian woody and mossy.

## What people often notice in aged Wuyi rock tea {#notice-aged}

A smoother, thicker liquor with less sharpness, the roast softened and the high aroma faded.
