## What oolong is {#definition}

Oolong is the partly oxidised tea, standing between green tea, which is not oxidised, and red tea, which is oxidised all the way. In Chinese it is also called qing cha, blue-green tea. The range inside it is wide: some oolongs sit close to green tea, floral and creamy, and others close to red tea, roasted, woody and dark-fruited. No single percentage describes the type, and the figures sellers give do not agree.

## How oolong is made {#making}

Oolong is made in a set order, and each step has a reason. The leaf is withered, often first in the sun and then indoors, so it goes soft enough to handle without breaking. It is then tossed or shaken in rounds with rests between them. The knocking bruises the leaf’s edges, which begin to oxidise, and the rests let the change spread slowly inward while the aroma builds. That is why a well-made oolong leaf is often green in the middle with a reddish edge. High heat, in a wok or a hot drum, then stops the change at the level the maker wants. The leaf is rolled, either into long twisted strips or, by rolling again and again in cloth, into tight balls. Last, it is dried, and many oolongs are then roasted.

## Oxidation and roast {#oxidation-and-roast}

How far the leaf is oxidised and how hard it is roasted are two separate choices. Roasting, over charcoal in the traditional heavy styles and often in several rounds over days or weeks, lowers the moisture so the tea keeps, and it mellows the tea and adds depth. A tea can be lightly oxidised and barely roasted, as most modern Anxi Tieguanyin and Taiwan high mountain tea are, or lightly oxidised and roasted hard, as traditional Tieguanyin was. Wuyi teas are labelled light, medium or full roast for this reason.

## The four families {#families}

Oolong is made in four main families, each named for where it comes from and how it is made, not for quality. North Fujian makes Wuyi rock tea, strip-rolled, usually the most oxidised and most roasted, prized for a lasting mineral quality the trade calls rock rhyme. South Fujian, led by Anxi and its Tieguanyin, makes ball-rolled tea. The Phoenix mountain area of Chaozhou in Guangdong makes Dan Cong, strip-rolled and mostly named for its aroma, which comes from the bush and not from added flavour. Taiwan, whose tea came from Fujian, makes mostly light to medium oolong, usually ball-rolled, with high mountain tea the most popular style.

## How oolong began {#history}

By the Qing dynasty oolong was an established craft in Fujian, refined in the Wuyi mountains and in Anxi during the 1700s, and the making of Tieguanyin in Anxi is dated to the early eighteenth century. The craft then spread to Chaozhou and to Taiwan, where oolong grew into an export tea in the second half of the nineteenth century. Where oolong first began, Wuyi or Anxi, is not settled, and the stories told about its name and its first makers are stories.

## Keeping and ageing oolong {#ageing}

Only well-made, well-roasted oolong is kept to age; the families most often aged are Wuyi rock tea, traditional Tieguanyin and roasted Taiwan oolong. Light, green-style oolong is a tea to drink fresh, and kept too long it goes flat or sour. Moisture is the danger: tea that was not dried and roasted enough takes up damp and turns grassy again. The heavier the roast, the more slowly the tea changes. Over years of storage good oolong is roasted again at low heat from time to time to drive off the moisture it has taken in, a skilled job that can cost some quality each round. Kept sealed, away from light, smells and damp, a roasted oolong softens with the years: the roast loses its edge, the body grows smoother and thicker, the liquor deepens towards red-brown, and the high floral aroma fades. There are no agreed ages at which oolong counts as aged.

## Brewing oolong {#brewing}

Oolong is most often brewed gongfu style, in a small gaiwan or clay pot with plenty of leaf and short steeps, and it stands many rounds. A common start is about 4 to 6 grams to 100 ml, with a first steep of roughly 5 to 15 seconds and 5 to 10 seconds more each round. Light, green-style oolong takes water a little off the boil, about 90 to 95°C; heavily oxidised, roasted and aged oolong takes water at a full boil. Many people pour a quick first rinse to wake tightly rolled leaf. In a larger pot, use less leaf and steep for a few minutes.

## What people often notice {#common-impressions}

In light, green-style oolong: floral and orchid notes, a creamy or buttery feel and a sweet aftertaste that lingers. In roasted and darker oolong: toasted grain and caramel, wood, stone fruit and a mineral, rock-like character.

## What people often notice in aged oolong {#notice-aged}

A smooth, mellow and sweet cup, often with dried fruit or plum and wood, sometimes something herbal, little astringency, and an amber to red-brown liquor.
