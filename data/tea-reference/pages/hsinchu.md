## Where Hsinchu is {#where-it-is}

Hsinchu is a county in the north west of Taiwan, with Taoyuan to the north, Miaoli to the south and the Taiwan Strait to the west. Most of it is hills and uplands, rising to the Xueshan and Dabajian ranges in the east. Most of its people are Hakka. Hsinchu City is a separate city and not part of the county, so on a tea record Hsinchu normally means the county, and a township such as Emei or Beipu is the finer place.

## Why people value Hsinchu tea {#why-people-value-it}

Hsinchu is the home of Oriental Beauty, the honey-sweet, fruity oolong that is the county's best known tea. It is a hill country tea, grown on low to middling ground rather than on the cold high mountains of central Taiwan, and the style depends on the climate of these hills in summer, when a small insect feeds on the young shoots. A buyer who sees Hsinchu on a tea is most likely looking at Oriental Beauty, and the place matters because the tea is hard to make well anywhere else.

## The tea townships {#tea-townships}

Within the county, Emei and Beipu are the two names the trade gives most often. They are neighbouring Hakka townships on the same river valley. Emei is described as the larger producer today, while Beipu has become better known as a heritage town with a historic old street and as a place to buy. Hengshan and Zhudong are also named as Oriental Beauty areas, and Guanxi is named in some accounts. Which township a tea comes from is worth reading on the label, because the county name covers all of them.

## A Hakka tea country {#hakka}

Tea in Hsinchu is largely a Hakka trade. Local Hakka families grow the leaf, make the tea and sell it, and many farms have passed down through generations, with family tea making reaching back to the Japanese period. Hakka tea culture here also includes lei cha, a ground tea drink with nuts and grains, which is associated with Beipu. Farm visits and tea trails are now part of how the county presents its tea.

## The summer leafhopper {#summer-leafhopper}

Oriental Beauty depends on a small green leafhopper that feeds on the young shoots in summer. Its bite makes the plant produce the compounds that give the tea its honey and ripe fruit character, so growers avoid spraying pesticide on these gardens. The buds are picked by hand, usually the bud and the next two leaves, and the leaf is oxidised heavily, close to a black tea, before it is dried. This is why Oriental Beauty is a summer tea in Hsinchu, and why it is more costly to grow than an oolong that does not need the insect.

## Oriental Beauty and its names {#oriental-beauty-names}

Oriental Beauty is also called bai hao oolong, meaning white tip oolong, after the pale buds in the leaf. In Hsinchu it has an older folk name, pengfeng cha, which means braggart's tea. Stories about how it got the name Oriental Beauty, and about it being sent to a queen, are told in many places, but they differ in detail and are not settled history.

## Hsinchu on a label {#on-a-label}

Hsinchu on a label names the county, and it does not by itself say which township or what kind of tea. Oriental Beauty is not made only in Hsinchu, and accounts say that production has spread to nearby areas such as Miaoli and Taoyuan, so the county name is part of what a buyer is paying for. A county name with a township, such as Emei or Beipu, says more. Whether Hsinchu makes other teas in quantity, such as green or red tea, is not established here, though small amounts of Baozhong, high mountain oolong and black tea are served at local tea events.
