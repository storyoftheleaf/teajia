## What Fujian white is {#definition}

Fujian is the home of white tea. Fuding and Zhenghe are its two leading counties, and Jianyang and Songxi make white tea too. Fuding is a coastal city in the north-east of the province, by the Zhejiang border, hilly over most of its land; its white tea is tied to Taimu Mountain, where the first mother tree and the first famous bud tea are placed. Fuding white tea is a protected origin name in China. Zhenghe is an inland mountain county further west.

## The bushes {#cultivars}

Fuding grows two named cultivars, Fuding Da Bai and Fuding Da Hao, and Fuding Silver Needle is made from their buds; Fuding Da Bai comes from Bailiu village in Diantou and has plump buds heavy with white down. Zhenghe has its own, Zhenghe Da Bai, used for its Silver Needle and White Peony. Both replaced, mostly from the late 1800s on, the older small-leaf local plants grown from seed, called Cai Cha.

## Fuding and Zhenghe {#fuding-and-zhenghe}

Fuding buds are plumper and downier than Zhenghe's. In the cup, Fuding is most often called the lighter, fresher, more floral style, and Zhenghe the fuller, thicker, mellower one. That is the trade's comparison of the two places, not a rule for every tea.

## Grades {#grades}

The trade sorts Fujian white tea by how the leaf was picked. Silver Needle is buds alone. White Peony is a bud with one or two young leaves. Gong Mei has more leaf than bud, and Shou Mei is the mature-leaf grade, with large leaves and few or no buds, often from later picks. Fuding also makes a separate new-craft white tea, xin gongyi, which is lightly rolled after withering and is darker in the cup.

## How Fujian white is made {#making}

Two steps make it: withering, then drying at low heat, with no fixing and no rolling for the classic grades. The leaf is withered in the sun, indoors in moving air, indoors with gentle warmth, or by a mix of these, as the weather and the leaf allow, usually for two to three days. It is then dried at low heat, over charcoal or by machine, until it keeps.

## White tea cakes {#cakes}

Pressed white tea is new. Cakes began in the 2000s and spread around 2010, because a cake is smaller and easier to keep and carry than loose leaf. A cake is made from one of several grades, White Peony, Gong Mei or Shou Mei, so its grade has to be read from its own description. Some cakes are pressed from leaf already aged loose for a few years, so the year on a cake can be the year it was pressed rather than picked.

## How Fujian white began {#history}

White tea in its present form is a Qing dynasty tea of the 1700s and 1800s, first from Fuding or Jianyang and from Zhenghe later in the 1800s. Fuding Silver Needle was first sold abroad around 1890. The stories of Taimu and older uses of the name white tea are legend, kept apart from this record.

## Keeping and ageing Fujian white {#ageing}

The trade says white tea is tea in its first year, medicine at three years and a treasure at seven; it is a saying, not a tested claim, and no agreed number of years makes a tea aged. With age it moves from fresh and sweet toward jujube, dried fruit, honey, wood and herbs, with a deeper, redder liquor and a rounder body. Keep it dry, clean and aired, away from strong smells and sunlight.

## Brewing Fujian white {#brewing}

Gongfu style, use about 1 gram of leaf to 15 to 25 ml of water, for example 5 grams in a 100 to 110 ml gaiwan; weigh it, since the leaf is light and bulky. Silver Needle and young, delicate tea take water below the boil, about 75 to 90°C; older, leafier or aged tea takes water at or near the boil. Start with a steep of 10 to 45 seconds and add a few seconds each round. An aged cake is often rinsed quickly first, and older pressed tea can be simmered on the stove, about 5 grams to 500 ml for 3 to 10 minutes; young, delicate tea is not.

## What people often notice {#common-impressions}

In young Fuding tea: a sweet cup, floral, fresh and brisk, with the scent of the down. Zhenghe tea is most often called thick and full.

## What people often notice in aged Fujian white {#notice-aged}

Jujube or dried dates, herbs and medicine, wood, honey and dried fruit, a deeper, redder liquor, a thicker, rounder body, and less lift in the scent than a young tea.
