## What Phoenix Dancong is {#definition}

Phoenix Dancong is the oolong of Phoenix Mountain, Fenghuang Shan, in Chao'an, Chaozhou, in eastern Guangdong. Wudong is the peak named most often as its heart. The dry leaf is a long strip, twisted tight, dark and glossy, not rolled into a ball.

## Single bush {#name}

Dancong means single bush: the leaf of one bush picked and made on its own, never mixed with others. The bushes are chosen from the old Phoenix Shuixian plants of the mountain. Today a named Dancong is mostly grown from cuttings or grafts of a chosen bush, so one name no longer means one tree, though some very old trees are still made on their own.

## Aroma types {#aromas}

Dancong is sorted and sold by its aroma type, named for a flower, fruit or spice the scent recalls. The scent comes from the bush and the making, not from anything added: an orchid or almond Dancong names a smell, not an ingredient. The trade speaks of the ten aroma types. Nine names turn up in every full list: Huangzhi, yellow sprig; Zhilan, orchid; Milan, honey orchid; Yulan, magnolia; Xingren, almond; Jianghua, ginger flower; Guihua, osmanthus; Yelai, night-blooming flower; and Rougui, cinnamon. The tenth is most often jasmine.

## Ya Shi Xiang {#ya-shi-xiang}

Ya Shi Xiang, duck dung aroma, is the market name for a very floral Dancong, and most lists of the ten leave it out even though it sells widely. It is also called Yinhua Xiang, silver flower or honeysuckle aroma, a name put forward in 2014 for a scent likened to the wild honeysuckle of the mountain; many sellers keep the old name. Its line is traced to Pingkengtou village. Three stories explain the name, all told as stories: that it was chosen to put thieves off the bush, that it comes from the yellow local soil called duck dung soil, and that duck droppings lay on the bush. The tea tastes nothing like its name: every account of the cup gives flowers, usually with milk or cream, honey or fruit.

## Mi Lan Xiang {#mi-lan-xiang}

Mi Lan Xiang, honey orchid, is one of the traditional ten aroma types and one of the most widely made, often named as a good first Dancong. It is described as honey-sweet with an orchid scent, with a cooked sweet potato note, and a liquor from golden amber to orange-red, depending on the roast. The one name covers a range of teas.

## How Dancong is made {#making}

The leaf is withered in the sun and cooled indoors, then shaken and rested in rounds; this is the step that bruises the leaf edge and builds the scent. It is fixed fast at high heat in a hot pan or drum, rolled into strips, dried, and roasted again as it is refined, sometimes over more than one round. Charcoal is the traditional fire. The roast is a main difference between teas: a light roast keeps the floral scent bright, a heavier one gives a deeper, sweeter cup with caramel in it.

## Old bushes and Song tea {#old-bushes}

Old bushes, laocong, are prized for depth and a lasting sweetness, and give little leaf; no age is agreed for when a bush counts as old. Song variety, songzhong, is a market label for the oldest named lines, Huangzhi above all, not a proven age. A legend gives the old trees their name: the last Song emperor, fleeing as a boy, was given tea on the mountain by a phoenix.

## The local tea {#history}

The old tea of the mountain was called bird-beak tea, and later Shuixian. Dancong is the everyday tea of Chaozhou, and gongfu brewing is tied to Chaozhou, where Chaozhou gongfu tea is still made with small vessels, set ways of pouring, and three cups.

## Keeping Dancong {#ageing}

Freshly roasted Dancong is rested for some months before it is at its best; how many months is not agreed. Keep it sealed, dry, cool and out of the light.

## Brewing Dancong {#brewing}

Brew it gongfu style in a small gaiwan or clay pot, with about 3 to 8 grams for 100 to 120 ml and hot water, about 88 to 100°C; Chinese accounts mostly say 95 to 100°C. A quick rinse is common. Steeps start at a few seconds and grow a few seconds each round, and a good Dancong gives about eight to fifteen. Too long, too hot or too much leaf and it turns bitter and astringent.

## What people often notice {#common-impressions}

Flowers above all, a sweetness that comes back after swallowing, a high scent that lasts, honey, often ripe fruit such as peach, and what the trade calls mountain rhyme, shan yun; a liquor from golden to orange-red or amber.
