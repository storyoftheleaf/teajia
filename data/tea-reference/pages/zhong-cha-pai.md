## What Zhong Cha Pai means on a cake {#definition}

Zhong Cha Pai means “Zhong Cha brand”. It is the name pu’er drinkers give to tea sold under the mark of the China National Tea Corporation, and it appears on ripe cakes, bricks and loose tea alike. It is a brand, not a grade of leaf: it says nothing about how fine or coarse the tea is, and it is not one of the leaf sorts that ripe pu’er is graded by. The company and its mark are covered on the China National Tea Corporation page.

## Ask when it was made {#era}

On a Zhong Cha tea, the year of manufacture tells you more than the mark does. Until the 1980s the big state factories made pu’er for the company, and the mark sat on teas that came out of one tightly run system. From the late 1980s the larger factories moved to their own names, and newer and smaller makers took up the Zhong Cha label, reportedly paying the company for the right. Two cakes with the same wrapper can therefore come from very different hands.

## Ripe tea under the mark {#ripe-cakes}

Ripe pu’er only began in the 1970s, so any ripe cake under the mark is from that decade or later. Most ripe Zhong Cha cakes people meet today date from the 1990s onward, the time when the label was being used by many makers, so the page for the mark cannot say how a particular ripe cake will taste. That depends on the factory, the recipe and how the cake has been stored.

## Look past the wrapper {#reading-the-label}

Buyers who know the mark check more than the outer paper. The inner ticket and the insert, the recipe code where there is one, and whether the stated year fits that recipe all help to place a cake. Wrappers often leave out the year, and the leaf is hard to trace, so a seller’s record of where the cake has been matters. Zhong Cha is also among the most copied pu’er names, and an old date is easy to claim, so a very low price for a tea said to be old is a reason to ask more.
