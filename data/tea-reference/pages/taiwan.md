## Where Taiwan is {#where-it-is}

Taiwan is an island off the coast of Fujian, across the strait from mainland China, and its tea came from Fujian. A tall mountain range runs down the middle and the east, with many peaks above 2,000 metres, and the tea gardens are mostly on the western side. The range shelters that side from ocean storms, the climate is warm and wet, and the higher gardens sit in cloud and mist for much of the day.

## What Taiwan is known for in tea {#known-for}

Taiwan is known above all for oolong. Its trade is built on rolled, bead-shaped oolong, much of it lightly oxidised, and on the high mountain teas grown above about 1,000 metres, where cool air, fog and short hours of direct sun slow the leaf and the tea tastes sweet and low in bitterness. Taiwan is also known for how many distinct styles it makes on one small island: roasted Dong Ding, the long-strip Baozhong, the heavily oxidised Oriental Beauty, and traditional Tieguanyin from Muzha. Black tea, green tea and newer oolong styles are made as well, but oolong is what the name Taiwan stands for.

## How the tea areas sit on the island {#areas}

The tea areas fall into a few groups. In the north, around Taipei and New Taipei, are Pinglin, home of Baozhong, and Muzha, home of Tieguanyin. Further along the north and northwest, Hsinchu and Miaoli are where Oriental Beauty is made. In the centre, Nantou is the largest tea county, with Lugu (Dong Ding), Shanlinxi and the red tea around Yuchi, and it is joined by the Taichung mountains of Lishan and Dayuling. In the southwest, Chiayi holds the Alishan area. The east coast, in Hualien and Taitung, is smaller and makes a wider mix of teas. The high mountain tea areas are mostly in Nantou, Chiayi and Taichung, and the older, lower areas are mostly in the north.

## How tea came to Taiwan {#history}

Tea came with settlers from Fujian, who brought plants and the craft of making oolong, and it grew into a trade for sale abroad in the nineteenth century, when oolong was sold overseas as Formosa Oolong. Under Japanese rule the island also turned to black tea, and in the late twentieth century more attention went to quality and to tea drunk at home, which is when high mountain oolong became so prized.

## Taiwan on a label {#on-a-label}

Taiwan on its own is a country, not a mountain, a county or a style. It tells you the tea was grown on the island and nothing more about where, at what height, from which cultivar or in which style. Two teas that both say Taiwan can be a roasted lowland oolong and a light high mountain oolong. Where a seller names a mountain or township, that is the fuller claim, and a bare Taiwan is the weaker one. The kind of tea, such as oolong, red tea or green tea, is a separate fact again.
