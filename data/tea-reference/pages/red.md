## What red tea is {#definition}

Red tea is the tea English calls black tea. The Chinese name follows the colour of the brewed cup, which is red; the English one follows the dry leaf, which is dark. It is the fully oxidised tea: once the leaf’s cells are broken, its own enzymes work on it until it has turned from green to reddish brown. The Chinese trade calls that step fermentation.

## How red tea is made {#making}

Red tea is made in four steps, each with a reason. The fresh leaf is withered, spread out until it loses water and goes soft enough to work. It is rolled, twisted and bruised so its juices and enzymes meet and the change can begin. It is left to oxidise in warmth and damp air, and the maker judges it by smell and colour, as the grassy smell turns floral or fruity and the leaf turns reddish brown. Then it is dried with hot air, which ends the change and makes the tea stable. Graded teas are then sorted and blended.

## Whole leaf and broken leaf {#whole-and-broken}

Red tea is made in two families. Whole-leaf strip tea, called gongfu in Chinese and orthodox in English, is rolled with care and is the choice for slow, attentive brewing. Broken-leaf tea, cut or crushed, gives its colour and strength fast, which is why it fills tea bags and suits milk tea.

## How red tea spread {#history}

By the trade’s own telling, red tea began in the Wuyi mountains of northern Fujian, around Tongmu, in the seventeenth century, though no record says exactly how or when it was first made. Dutch traders and then the British East India Company carried it to Europe under the names Bohea and Souchong. In the nineteenth century the craft spread to other Chinese provinces, among them Anhui, where Keemun began late in the Qing dynasty, and the British, having taken Chinese plants and tea makers to India, planted red tea in Assam and later Darjeeling. Taiwan’s red tea dates from the Japanese period, at Yuchi near Sun Moon Lake, and Yunnan’s Dian Hong from the late 1930s.

## Keeping red tea {#ageing}

Red tea has no tradition of ageing. It is a tea to drink fresh: sealed, it keeps its quality for about a year and a half to three years, and after that it turns flatter.

## Brewing red tea {#brewing}

Red tea takes water near the boil, about 90 to 100°C; hotter water draws out its aroma and flavour more fully, but its bitterness faster too. In a pot or mug, use about a rounded spoon of leaf for each cup and steep three to five minutes, less for broken leaf and a little less, and a little cooler, for a light, delicate tea. Left too long, it turns bitter and drying, because time and heat pull more tannins from the leaf.

## What people often notice {#common-impressions}

A sweet cup, often with honey, floral or fruity notes, malt in many, and a brisk strength in the stronger teas. The liquor runs red, bright to deep.
