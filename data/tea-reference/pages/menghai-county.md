## Why people value Menghai {#why-people-value-it}

For its trees, above all. Menghai’s old gardens grow in and beside living forest, in warm, wet hills, and many of the trees are centuries old. That old-tree leaf is what tea people mean by Menghai’s mountain character: a strong cup whose bitterness turns quickly into a sweet, lasting finish, the quality the trade calls huigan, most of all on Bulang, while Nannuo gives a softer, more floral tea. The factory history made the name famous, but the trees are why buyers seek out its mountains.

## Western Xishuangbanna {#scope}

Menghai County is the core of pu’er country in western Xishuangbanna. Nannuo, Bulang, Mengsong, Hekai, Pasha and Bada are distinct mountain areas within it, each with its own tea, not interchangeable names for Menghai. [cite:teadb-menghai-county]

## A forceful reputation {#common-character}

Menghai raw pu’er has a reputation for strength and bitterness, set against the gentler teas of eastern Xishuangbanna. Treat that as a regional convention rather than a rule: tree age, craft, blend, age and storage all make large differences within the county. [cite:teadb-menghai-county]

## Why the name is so well known {#history}

Menghai’s fame also comes from its factory. Menghai Tea Factory was founded in the 1940s, and as pu’er grew into a large state industry the county’s leaf went into factory blends, so many benchmark raw and ripe cakes trace back to Menghai. [cite:teadb-menghai-county]

## Mengsong and Naka {#mengsong-naka}

Naka is a village in Mengsong, one of Menghai County’s tea-growing areas. [cite:teadb-menghai-county]

## Where ripe pu’er began {#ripe-origins}

Ripe pu’er was born in the early 1970s, when Yunnan’s state factories learned to speed up ageing by wet piling the leaf, and Menghai Tea Factory was one of the first to master it. Its 7572 cake became the benchmark ripe recipe, the cup other ripe teas are still judged against. The number reads as the recipe itself: 75 for the year it was first made, 7 for the main leaf grade in the blend, 2 for Menghai.

## Who tends the mountains {#peoples}

Menghai’s tea mountains are not one community. Bulang Mountain is the home of the Bulang people, and the Bulang are widely credited as some of the first to grow and make tea here. Nannuo is mostly tended by Hani families today (the Aini branch), although the Bulang are said to have planted its earliest trees. In Mengsong, most of the old gardens were planted by Lahu villagers. Who planted and who picks shapes each mountain’s gardens and its tea, so a mountain name is a better guide to a cup than the county name.

## Menghai the place, Menghai Tea Factory the maker {#county-and-factory}

Menghai on a label can mean the county where the leaf grew, or Menghai Tea Factory, which sits in the county and is part of the Dayi group. They are not the same claim. The factory buys and blends leaf from several Menghai mountains, and some of its ripe tea uses leaf from outside the county, such as Lincang. Its recipes and its history belong to its own cakes. A Menghai tea from a village or a small maker is not a factory tea, and a factory cake is not a single mountain unless it says so.

## Old trees, left to grow {#old-gardens}

What sets the old Menghai gardens apart is how they are kept. The trees are allowed to grow tall, with little pruning, and farmers use little or no fertiliser or spray. They give far less leaf than pruned plantation bushes, which are picked again and again through the season. People who buy old-garden Menghai tea are paying for that slow, low-yield leaf, and it is why the same mountain can taste so different from its plantation tea.
