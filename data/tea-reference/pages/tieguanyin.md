## What Tieguanyin is {#definition}

Tieguanyin is the name of a tea cultivar and of the oolong made from it. The tea comes from Anxi County in southern Fujian, where the name is protected as Anxi Tieguanyin, and the trade names Xiping township in Anxi as the place it began; Gande is another of the best-known Anxi townships. The name joins iron and Guanyin, the bodhisattva of mercy. The finished leaf is usually rolled into tight balls that open slowly in the cup. Outside Anxi, in Taiwan above all, the name can mean a way of making the tea rather than the plant.

## Guanyin rhyme {#guanyin-rhyme}

The quality the trade prizes in good Tieguanyin is called Guanyin rhyme, guanyin yun. It is a tasting word for the whole effect: an orchid or floral fragrance, a thick, sweet taste, and an aftertaste and throat feeling that lingers. Good Tieguanyin is said to stay fragrant after seven infusions, a trade saying rather than a tested limit.

## How Tieguanyin is made {#making}

The leaf is picked when the shoot has opened, withered in the late-afternoon sun, cooled, then shaken and rested in rounds. Shaking bruises the leaf edges so they turn red while the middle stays green, the green leaf with a red rim. The leaf is fixed in a hot pan or drum and rolled. To make the balls, it is wrapped in cloth, rolled and pressed, dried a little, and wrapped again, several times over, each round tightening it. It is finished with a slow drying at low heat.

## Light, roasted and aged {#styles}

Anxi Tieguanyin is sorted by style. The light-fragrance kind, qingxiang, is lightly oxidised and lightly roasted or not roasted at all, with a fresh orchid fragrance and a green-gold liquor. The strong-fragrance kind, nongxiang, is more oxidised and roasted, with a darker leaf, a gold to amber liquor and toasted, caramel and fruit notes. A third kind, chenxiang or old Tie, is Tieguanyin kept for years. The roasted style is the older one; the light green style is newer and took over in Anxi from the 1990s, and the roasted style is said to be slowly coming back.

## Grades {#grades}

Anxi Tieguanyin has a national standard, GB 19598, first in force in 2005 and since replaced by a 2025 version. Brands print grade words such as tèjí, special grade, and yījí, first grade, in their names and listings.

## Osmanthus Tieguanyin {#osmanthus}

Osmanthus Tieguanyin is a scented tea. Fresh osmanthus flowers are layered with the finished tea, which takes up their scent, and the tea is then dried again.

## How Tieguanyin began {#history}

Two stories are told of how Tieguanyin began, both set in Xiping and both folk accounts, not records. In one, a farmer named Wei dreams of Guanyin, is led to a tea plant and grows it, in the Yongzheng reign of the 1720s and 1730s. In the other, a scholar named Wang finds a tea plant and presents its tea to the Qianlong emperor, who names it. Anxi sources date the making of oolong in Anxi to about 1725 to 1735.

## Keeping and ageing Tieguanyin {#ageing}

Light, low-roast Tieguanyin is for drinking fresh and goes stale fast; the heavier charcoal-roasted kind is steadier and is the kind kept for years. Freshly roasted tea tastes of fire and is rested, months or years, until the fire settles. With age the tea softens: the fire fades, the liquor deepens, the body grows rounder and smoother and less sharp, and well-aged roasted tea often shows a soft fruity sourness. Keep it sealed, dry, dark and cool, away from strong smells, and look at it now and then; roasted tea does not need the refrigerator, which is for the light style.

## Brewing Tieguanyin {#brewing}

Tieguanyin is usually brewed gongfu style with water at or near the boil, about 95 to 100°C; the light green style is often taken a little cooler, about 85 to 95°C. Use about 4 to 8 grams of leaf in a 100 to 150 ml gaiwan or pot, about a third full. A quick rinse, poured off at once, is common. Steeps start short, about 10 to 30 seconds, and grow a few seconds longer each round, and a good Tieguanyin gives about six to eight. In a larger pot, use about 2.5 to 5 grams for 240 to 350 ml and steep two to three minutes.

## What people often notice {#common-impressions}

In the light style: orchid and other flowers, a fresh, clean cup and a sweet aftertaste. In the roasted style: toast and charcoal, caramel or honey, stone or dried fruit, nuts, and a liquor that runs from gold to amber.

## What people often notice in aged Tieguanyin {#notice-aged}

A smoother, rounder, softer cup with the fire gone, a liquor turned orange-red or reddish-brown, and often a soft fruity sourness.
