## What Taiwan oolong is {#definition}

Taiwan oolong is the oolong of Taiwan, whose tea plants and craft came from Fujian. Most of it is lightly to moderately oxidised and rolled into balls, and Qingxin oolong is the main cultivar, with Jinxuan the other common one. The island makes several distinct styles: high mountain tea, Dong Ding from Lugu in Nantou, Baozhong from the north, and Oriental Beauty from Hsinchu and Miaoli.

## High mountain tea {#high-mountain}

High mountain tea, gao shan cha, is tea from gardens at about 1,000 metres and higher. It is a label for where the tea grew, not a cultivar or a recipe. The best-known areas are Alishan in Chiayi, Lishan in Taichung, Shanlinxi and Yushan, and Dayuling. At altitude the leaf grows slowly and comes out thick and fleshy, and the trade explains its sweetness and low bitterness by that slow growth. The usual high mountain tea is lightly oxidised, rolled into balls and left unroasted or only lightly roasted.

## Dong Ding {#dong-ding}

Dong Ding oolong comes from Lugu Township in Nantou County, and its name is also used for the style made elsewhere. The leaf is withered in the sun and indoors with tossing, fixed, rolled into balls in cloth, dried, and then roasted. Traditional Dong Ding is roasted over charcoal, longan wood among the fuels; electric roasters are used too. It grows near or below the high mountain line, so it is not counted as high mountain tea.

## Baozhong {#baozhong}

Baozhong is made in the north of Taiwan, mostly in Pinglin and the nearby Wenshan area, and is the most lightly oxidised Taiwan oolong. Unlike most Taiwan oolong it is left as a long twisted strip, not rolled into balls, and it is normally dried rather than roasted. The name means wrapped kind, from the old habit of wrapping the tea in paper.

## Oriental Beauty {#oriental-beauty}

Oriental Beauty is made from buds and leaves bitten by a small green leafhopper. The bite starts the leaf’s change on the bush and is the root of the tea’s character, which is why its growers avoid insecticide on those fields. It is picked in early summer, is far more oxidised than other Taiwan oolong, close to red tea, and comes mainly from Hsinchu County, around Beipu and Emei, and from Miaoli. Its older name is Pengfeng tea, bragging tea.

## Muzha Tieguanyin {#muzha}

Muzha, in southern Taipei, is the home of Taiwan’s traditional Tieguanyin. The tea master Zhang Naimiao is credited with bringing the cultivar there from Anxi, with a clansman, during the years of Japanese rule in Taiwan; the year is told differently. The traditional Muzha style is a more oxidised, charcoal-roasted tea, its leaf wrapped in cloth and rolled again and again.

## How Taiwan oolong grew {#history}

Oolong came to Taiwan with settlers from Fujian and was grown for sale in the nineteenth century. In the 1860s the Scottish merchant John Dodd, working with the Taipei merchant Li Chunsheng, sold it abroad as Formosa Oolong, and tea became one of the island’s leading exports, which it stayed under Japanese rule. In the late twentieth century the market turned inward: more Taiwan tea was drunk at home than sold abroad, and high mountain oolong became one of the island’s most prized teas.

## Keeping and ageing Taiwan oolong {#ageing}

Taiwan has a tradition of keeping oolong for years, called lao cha, old tea. The tea chosen for keeping starts well roasted and dry, and it is roasted again at low heat from time to time to drive out the moisture it takes in; how often is a matter of the keeper’s judgement. Stored cool, dry, dark and sealed, it loses its bitterness and astringency with the years, the cup turns smooth, round and sweet, and the liquor deepens through amber towards red-brown. No standard says how many years make an oolong old; those who give a figure put it somewhere between ten and thirty years.

## Brewing Taiwan oolong {#brewing}

Ball-rolled Taiwan oolong takes hot water, about 90 to 100°C, a fairly generous amount of leaf and short steeps; the balls open slowly and give many infusions. Roasted Dong Ding takes water from about 95°C to a full boil. Aged oolong takes very hot water, in a pot that holds its heat, clay above all. Oriental Beauty is the exception and takes cooler water, about 80 to 95°C.

## What people often notice {#common-impressions}

In light high mountain tea: a sweet, floral cup, often creamy or buttery, with little bitterness and a long sweet aftertaste. In roasted Dong Ding: caramel, ripe fruit, nuts and a toasted aroma. In Baozhong: floral notes and a clear, bright liquor. In Oriental Beauty: ripe fruit and honey, with little bitterness.

## What people often notice in aged Taiwan oolong {#notice-aged}

A smooth, mellow, thick cup with little astringency, often plum or prune, sometimes something like medicinal herbs or ginseng, and an amber to red-brown liquor.
