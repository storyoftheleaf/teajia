## What Dian Hong is {#definition}

Dian Hong is red tea from Yunnan, made from the province’s large-leaf tea trees. Its home is Fengqing County in Lincang, in the south-west of the province, and it is also made in other parts of Lincang and in Menghai, Pu’er, Baoshan and Dehong. Golden buds through the dry leaf are the mark of the style.

## Why the large leaf matters {#large-leaf}

Yunnan’s large-leaf trees give a plump leaf with many buds, and a strong cup. The trade ties that strength to the leaf’s high polyphenol content, which is also why Dian Hong stands up to milk. The buds turn golden as the tea is made, so a tea heavy in buds shows more gold.

## How Dian Hong began {#history}

When the war cut China off from its eastern tea regions, the country needed a new red tea to sell abroad, and the national tea company looked to Yunnan’s large leaf. Feng Shaoqiu reached Shunning, now Fengqing, in late 1938 and made a trial batch from one bud and two leaves. An experimental factory followed there in 1939, the start of today’s Fengqing factory. For decades nearly all Dian Hong went abroad; it was sold widely at home only from the 1980s.

## How Dian Hong is made {#making}

Dian Hong follows the steps of every red tea: the leaf is withered, then rolled to break its cells so oxidation can begin, then left to oxidise until it turns from green to reddish brown, and then dried with heat, which fixes it and keeps the leaf golden. It is then sorted into grades. There are two broad kinds: gongfu, whole or strip leaf, and broken. The gongfu grades use one bud with one to three leaves, and teas of buds alone, sold as golden bud or golden needle, sit at the top. A grade name says nothing about where the leaf grew or how old the trees were.

## Old-tree and sun-dried red {#old-tree-and-sun-dried}

Two newer branches sell under the Yunnan red name. Old-tree red is made from old tea trees; it is priced higher, and there is no agreed definition or standard for it. Sun-dried red is red tea whose last drying is done in the strong spring sun of the mountains rather than over heat, a choice of process and not a separate kind of tea. The trade does not agree whether either should be called Dian Hong.

## Keeping Dian Hong {#ageing}

Dian Hong changes in storage, but the trade treats it as best in its first one to three years. It is kept for freshness rather than for age.

## Brewing Dian Hong {#brewing}

Dian Hong takes hot water, roughly 90 to 100°C. A common start is about 5 grams to 100 ml, with short steeps that lengthen slowly as the leaf opens.

## What people often notice {#common-impressions}

A full, sweet cup, often described as malty, with chocolate or cocoa and honey notes and sometimes something floral. The liquor runs golden to bright red, and a good grade has little bitterness.
