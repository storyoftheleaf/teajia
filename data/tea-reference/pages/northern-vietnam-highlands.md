## Where the northern highlands are {#where-it-is}

The northern Vietnam highlands are the mountain provinces along and near the border with China, where old Shan Tuyet tea trees grow. Ha Giang and Lao Cai are the two most often named for this tea, and Yen Bai and Son La are usually listed with them. Within Ha Giang the trade names Hoang Su Phi and Vi Xuyen, within Lao Cai the Bac Ha area, and Suoi Giang is the best known grove on the Yen Bai side. Gardens sit high, mostly above about 1,000 metres, and the range is cool and misty.

## Shan Tuyet old trees {#old-trees}

Shan Tuyet means snow mountain, a name that comes from the fine white down on the buds. It names a family of large-leaf Shan tea trees and not one single cultivar. Many of the trees are old, with some said to be several hundred years, though ages are mostly local estimates and not measured. Mông and Dao communities look after many of the groves, and a number of old stands have been listed as Vietnam heritage trees. People value the tea for these old, lightly managed trees on forested slopes, in the same way they value old-tree leaf in Yunnan.

## Why it is set beside Yunnan {#beside-yunnan}

The highlands share the large-leaf tea trees, the mountain climate and the forest setting of the Yunnan hills just over the border. For that reason the trade often compares Shan Tuyet leaf with the leaf used for pu'er, and some sellers press it into cakes in the pu'er manner. How far back tea crossed the border, and how much of it is traded across it today, is not established here. The Vietnamese tea is its own product from its own country, and a comparison with Yunnan does not make it a Yunnan tea.

## Ripe tea from the highlands {#ripe-tea}

Ripe, pu'er style tea is made here from old-tree leaf, mostly in Ha Giang, and some Lao Cai makers also list a pu'er style tea. Sellers describe a medium fermentation, which they say gives a softer cup than ripe tea from Yunnan, with woody, earthy and nutty notes and a sweet finish. Some keep it for several years in the humid mountain climate. The stated age and the trees behind a given cake are usually the seller's own account, so they are read as that and not as a standard.

## The pu'er name on a label {#on-a-label}

Whether a ripe cake from Vietnam may be called pu'er is not agreed. Many people hold that the name belongs to tea grown in Yunnan, and some sellers of these teas say so themselves, calling the tea a Vietnamese dark tea made in the pu'er style. Others use the pu'er name freely. A label that says Ha Giang or Shan Tuyet gives a place and a tree type, and not a legal grade, a recipe or an age.
