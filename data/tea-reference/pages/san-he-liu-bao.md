## The three cranes {#three-cranes}

San He means “Three Cranes.” Wuzhou Tea Factory traces the birds on its trademark to Hegang Tower beside the factory. The works opened for the export trade in 1953. The factory registered the Three Cranes mark in October 1991, nearly four decades later. [cite:mofcom-three-cranes]

## What buyers wanted from the leaf {#export-buyers}

In July 1959, Guangxi’s trade department circulated an account from tea makers in Buyi, in Cangwu County. Export buyers wanted a brighter, deeper red in the cup and a tighter twist to the leaf. The makers lowered the heat of the firing pan and rolled the leaves more firmly. The circular records how they changed their work in response to buyers overseas. [cite:guangxi-archives-liubao]

## Water and the Wuzhou pile {#cold-water-piling}

Wuzhou Tea Factory dates its cold-water piling method to 1958. Workers moisten the leaves and heap them for fermentation. Zhou Mingliang was among the first technicians to practise it. In 1963, Qin Jiquan joined the factory and learned it on shift. When He Meizhen joined the factory in 1988, Qin was one of the senior technicians who taught her. [cite:mofcom-three-cranes]

## The basket makers {#basket-weavers}

Wuzhou Tea Factory uses handwoven bamboo baskets for its Three Cranes teas. The weave holds its shape and lets air pass through while the tea rests. The supply cooperative describes a network of village weavers, including older residents who earn an income making the baskets. [cite:china-coop-san-he-baskets]

## A warehouse built for aging {#aging-warehouse}

In 2019 reporters stepped into Wuzhou Tea Factory’s brick-and-timber aging warehouse. Work on it began in 1953, the year the factory opened. Factory staff explained that the porous wooden boards help moderate moisture and temperature while the tea rests after fermentation. [cite:cns-wuzhou-aging-warehouse]
