## Where Fujian is {#where-it-is}

Fujian is a coastal province in south-east China, facing Taiwan across the strait. Most of it is mountain and hill country, with short rivers running to the sea, and the provincial capital is Fuzhou. Tea grows in nearly every part of the province, and four areas stand out: the Wuyi Mountains in the north-west, Anxi in the south, Fuding on the north-east coast and Zhenghe in the north. Each has its own page.

## Why so many teas come from one province {#why-diverse}

Fujian is warm and wet, with a subtropical monsoon climate, mist on the hills and mild winters. Its mountains and valleys cut the province into pockets with their own weather, and before modern roads the makers in each pocket worked on their own. Over centuries each area settled on its own plant varieties and its own way of making tea. That is the reason sellers give for Fujian making more distinct styles than any other Chinese province, and it is why Fujian on its own says little about what a tea is.

## What Fujian is known for {#known-for}

Fujian is known for oolong, white tea and black tea, and it also makes green tea and scented tea. The best-known names are Wuyi rock oolong such as Da Hong Pao, Tieguanyin from Anxi, Fuding and Zhenghe white tea, Lapsang Souchong from Wuyi, and jasmine tea from Fuzhou. Smaller oolong traditions sit beside them, such as the pressed square cakes of Zhangping and Yongchun Fo Shou. These are different teas from different hands, so one of them is never a description of the whole province.

## The four areas, and how they differ {#areas}

The Wuyi Mountains make heavily roasted rock oolong and the original smoked black tea. Anxi makes lighter, floral oolong, above all Tieguanyin. Fuding and Zhenghe make most of Fujian's white tea, Fuding on the cooler, damp coast and Zhenghe inland and colder in winter, and the two are usually described as having slightly different tastes. The trade treats these as separate worlds of tea that happen to share a province, with different cultivars, craft and cup.

## Where black tea and oolong began {#history}

Fujian is widely named as the birthplace of black tea, and Lapsang Souchong, from Tongmu village in the Wuyi Mountains, is the tea usually given as the first. Oolong is also traced to the Wuyi area, and its making is said to have spread from there to Anxi in southern Fujian, to Guangdong and then to Taiwan. How early either began is not settled, and the dates given vary from one account to the next. What the sources agree on is the place, with Wuyi at the centre. Tea shipped from this area to Europe in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries was sold as Bohea, a spelling of the local pronunciation of Wuyi.

## The word tea {#word-tea}

The English word tea comes from the southern Fujian pronunciation of the Chinese word, close to te, which Dutch traders carried home from the coast around Xiamen. Languages that received tea by sea mostly say some form of tea, and those that received it overland say some form of cha. It is a small reason people link Fujian with the early overseas tea trade.

## Fujian on a label {#on-a-label}

Fujian on a label is a province, not a style. It can sit on oolong, white, black, green or scented tea, and the teas differ in leaf, roast, oxidation and price. It also does not say which of the main areas the tea came from. For that, look for Wuyi, Anxi, Fuding or Zhenghe, and for the tea's own name. A white tea that names Fujian only may come from either of the two white tea counties, or from another county that makes it.
