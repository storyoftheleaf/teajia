## What green tea is {#definition}

Green tea is the tea whose leaf is never allowed to oxidise. Soon after picking, the leaf is heated, and the heat stops the leaf’s own enzymes before they can turn it brown, so it keeps its green colour and its fresh, leafy taste. That sets it apart from red tea, where the change is let run to the end, and from oolong, where it is stopped halfway. Green tea is made in many provinces of China, Zhejiang, Jiangsu and Anhui among them, and in Japan, Taiwan and elsewhere.

## How green tea is made {#making}

Green tea is made in three steps, and each has a reason. First the leaf is fixed, heated fast in a hot pan or drum, or with steam, to stop the enzymes; the Chinese trade calls this killing the green, and it is the step that makes the tea green. Then the leaf is rolled, twisted and pressed, so it takes its shape and lets out a little juice. Last it is dried, by pan, oven heat or sun, until only a few percent of water is left, so it keeps and does not go mouldy.

## The main styles {#styles}

Green tea is sorted by how it is fixed and how it is dried. Pan-fired tea is dried in the pan and is the usual Chinese way; West Lake Longjing from Zhejiang, flat and straight, is the best-known example, and Biluochun from Jiangsu is curled into small snails. Steamed tea is fixed with steam, which is the usual Japanese way, and it gives a deeper green leaf and a grassier, sometimes seaweed-like taste. Oven-dried tea is dried over gentle heat after fixing, which leaves the leaf looser and keeps more of its white down. Sun-dried tea is dried in sunlight.

## Anhui green tea {#anhui}

Anhui is one of the main green tea provinces of China. Its best-known green tea is Huangshan Maofeng, from the Huangshan mountains: a slightly curled leaf covered in fine white down, fixed and then dried over gentle heat rather than in a pan, which is why it counts as oven-dried and not pan-fired.

## Keeping green tea {#ageing}

Green tea is a tea to drink fresh. The sources read give its best time as within about a year of picking, and a shelf life of a year or two when it is sealed. After that it goes flat: the fresh smell fades and the cup turns dull. Nothing read describes a tradition of ageing green tea, and it does not mature the way pu’er or white tea can. Keep it sealed, dark, dry and away from strong smells. If a green tea is sold as old, what counts is how it was kept, because the type itself does not promise anything.

## Brewing green tea {#brewing}

Green tea takes water well below the boil, about 80°C, a little cooler for the youngest leaf and a little warmer for older, larger leaf. Boiling water draws out bitterness and dries the cup. A common start is about 2 grams of leaf to 100 ml, in a glass or a small gaiwan, with short steeps of half a minute to two minutes, and the same leaf can be brewed a few times.

## What people often notice {#common-impressions}

A fresh, clean cup that many call crisp, with a sweet, soft finish, and a pale green to yellow-green liquor. The smell runs from fresh leaf to flowers, and steamed teas lean grassy or seaweed-like. Too much heat or too long a steep turns the cup bitter and drying.
