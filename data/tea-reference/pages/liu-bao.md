## What Liu Bao is {#definition}

Liu Bao is a dark tea from Cangwu County, near Wuzhou in Guangxi, named for the town of Liubao where it began. Like every dark tea, its leaf is piled after it is made, so that warmth, moisture and microbes turn it darker and softer, and then it is packed and left to age.

## Who drank it, and why {#nanyang-trade}

Liu Bao was made for a market far from home. Chinese miners and labourers in Malaya and across Southeast Asia drank it, above all in the tin mines, and the trade held that it eased the heat and damp of their work and settled the stomach; it was also a taste of home. The tea went by boat down the river to Wuzhou, on to Guangzhou, and out by sea. In the Qing dynasty it was already counted among China’s famous teas.

## The farmhouse way {#making-traditional}

The older, farmhouse way of making Liu Bao leans on steam and time. The leaf is steamed to soften it, then heaped damp so it darkens and loses its bitterness, and in most accounts it is steamed again before it is pressed. It is dried slowly over a wood or pine fire, which can leave a faint smoke and keeps the leaf sound enough to age. Then it is packed tight into bamboo baskets and rested in a cool, damp, dark store for one to three years before it is sold.

## The factory way {#making-factory}

In the 1950s the Wuzhou tea factory worked out cold-water piling, around 1958: the leaf is wetted and heaped at a modest warmth and turned until it has darkened, a steadier route than the farmhouse steaming. Factory tea, too, is packed in baskets and aged before sale.

## How the trade fell and returned {#history}

From the late 1930s war and blockade cut the river trade, and in the decades that followed production fell further as the planned economy changed who could buy and sell tea, and later as farmers left tea for other work. When the Malaysian tin mines declined, much old stock was left sitting in warehouses in Malaysia and Hong Kong. That forgotten stock is where most very old Liu Bao comes from today.

## Young Liu Bao {#age-young}

Under about five years a Liu Bao is still young. Its cup is paler, yellow to orange, and it can carry a faint smell of the pile and a little brightness or bitterness that age will soften.

## Five to ten years {#age-settling}

Between about five and ten years the aged character begins to show. The pile smell has gone, the cup deepens toward red, and the woody, sweet notes the trade looks for start to come through.

## Ten years and more {#age-aged}

After about ten years the trade counts a Liu Bao as aged. The cup turns amber to deep red-brown, thick and smooth, with woody and herbal notes, and decades-old tea is often said to take on ginseng and camphor. How it was stored matters as much as how long, and the trade does not agree whether it keeps improving for decades or peaks sooner.

## Old stock {#old-stock}

Liu Bao that is several decades old is mostly stock that sat in Malaysia or Hong Kong after the mining trade ended. It is prized and hard to date, so the trade reads its age from the basket, the labels and the papers packed with it, and the place it was stored gives each lot its own character.

## Brewing Liu Bao {#brewing}

Liu Bao takes very hot or boiling water, because its leaf is dense and gives up its depth slowly. Rinse it once or twice and pour the rinse away. Use about 5 to 8 grams to 100 ml, start with steeps of five to fifteen seconds and add a few seconds each round; a good Liu Bao gives ten cups or more.

## What people often notice {#common-impressions}

A sweet, smooth cup, woody and herbal, which the trade sums up as red, thick, aged and mellow. The note most often named is betel nut, faint in younger tea and clearer with age.

## What people often notice in aged Liu Bao {#notice-aged}

A deep red, thick cup with woody, herbal and betel nut notes; tea several decades old is often described with ginseng and camphor.
