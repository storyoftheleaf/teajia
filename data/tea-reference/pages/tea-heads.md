## What tea heads are {#definition}

Tea heads are dense clumps of leaf that stick together while ripe pu’er is wet piled. They are sorted out after piling and sold on their own, loose or pressed, apart from the numbered grades of loose ripe tea.

## How they form {#formation}

Pectin from the leaf, with heat and the weight of the pile, glues leaf into lumps, and microbes keep working inside them. After piling, workers pick the lumps out and try to loosen them. Lumps too tight to break without crushing the leaf are set aside as tea heads. They are about 0.8 to 1.5 percent of a batch.

## Why "old" {#old}

The word old does not mean old raw leaf. Fresh tea heads smell strongly of the pile, so they are usually left some years before drinking, until that smell fades, and then they are called old tea heads.

## What people often notice {#common-impressions}

Because of the pectin, tea heads give many infusions and a thick, smooth, sweet cup with little bitterness. Sellers name date and woody notes. They are brewed with a rinse first, and they often do not open fully.
