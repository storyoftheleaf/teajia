## What dark tea is {#definition}

Dark tea is Chinese tea whose leaf, after it is heated and rolled, is left to change in warmth and moisture, where microbes and the leaf’s own chemistry slowly darken and mellow it. Because that change comes after the leaf is made, the trade calls it post-fermented. In Chinese it is hei cha, “black tea”, which is not the English black tea; that tea is red tea, hong cha, in Chinese.

## Why it was made {#why}

Dark tea was made for people who lived far from where tea grows: Tibet, Mongolia, China’s north-west and, later, Central Asia and Russia. The trade called it border tea. The tea had to survive months on the road, so it was pressed into bricks, cakes and logs, or packed tight in baskets. A dense, even shape is easier to carry, stack and strap to a pack animal, and it keeps better than loose leaf. Some dark tea is still sold loose.

## How dark tea is made {#making}

Most dark tea shares four steps, each with a reason. The leaf is heated first, the kill-green, to stop its own enzymes from browning it; coarse leaf is often sprinkled with a little water so it heats evenly. It is rolled gently, because the large, mature leaf used for dark tea is tough and breaks if it is worked hard. It is then piled, and in the pile warmth, moisture and microbes do the darkening and mellowing, so the heap is turned to keep its heat in range. Finally it is dried, which stops the change and makes the tea stable enough to keep. Many styles then steam the leaf and press it, or pack it into baskets, and let it age.

## How the trade grew {#history}

The first records of dark tea by name are from the Ming dynasty, in Hunan around Anhua and in Sichuan, and in the late Ming it became a state-managed tea sent to the north-west in exchange for horses. In the Qing dynasty merchants carried the craft to more regions and more shapes, and some styles were made for new markets: Guangxi’s Liu Bao, for one, went to the tin mines of Southeast Asia. In the twentieth century state factories took over much of the making and settled the brick shapes that are sold today.

## Dark tea and age {#ageing}

Dark tea is made to be kept, and the trade values it more with age. Over years it turns darker and redder in the cup and grows mellower and sweeter. How fast it changes depends on how it is stored, and the trade does not agree on one way: some keep it cool and dry for a slow, clean change, others warmer and more humid for a faster, deeper one. When a tea counts as young or aged differs from style to style, so each style sets its own measure.

## Brewing dark tea {#brewing}

Dark tea takes boiling water. Its leaf is mature and dense, so it gives up its flavour slowly and is hard to overbrew. Rinse it first with boiling water and pour that off within seconds: the rinse wakes the leaf and washes off dust and any stale smell from storage, and an old tea may take a second rinse. In a small pot use plenty of leaf, about 7 grams to 120 ml, then steep very briefly at first and a little longer each time; a good dark tea gives many cups.

## Simmering older dark tea {#brewing-simmer}

Older or coarse dark tea is often simmered rather than steeped: the leaf goes into a pot of water, comes to a gentle boil and simmers for a few minutes. Thick leaf and long-aged tea release their flavour slowly, and the steady heat gives a fuller, smoother cup than short steeps do.

## Breaking a pressed dark tea {#brewing-pressed}

From a brick, cake or basket, ease the leaf off along the edge or from the top with a pick rather than snapping it. Whole pieces give up their flavour steadily; crumbs release it all at once and cloud the cup.

## What people often notice {#common-impressions}

A smooth, mellow cup with low bitterness and a deep red colour, often sweet and woody or earthy, sometimes with herbal or medicinal notes. A young dark tea can still smell of the pile or of storage; an aged one is usually redder, sweeter and thicker.
