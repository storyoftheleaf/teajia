## Where Anxi is {#where-it-is}

Anxi is a county in southern Fujian, in the prefecture of Quanzhou, inland from the coast and north of Xiamen. It sits on the southern side of the Daiyun mountains. The county town lies in a low basin, and the land rises into hills and ridges all round it. Anxi is the home of Tieguanyin and one of the main oolong counties of China.

## Why tea does well here {#why-tea}

Anxi is warm, wet and misty, with mild winters and rain through most of the year. The mountains are weathered granite, which breaks down into acidic, free-draining red and yellow soil, the kind tea likes. Farmers build terraced gardens on the slopes, keep forest on the ridges above, and farm the foothills and river valleys below. The higher gardens have cooler nights, more fog and slower growth, and sellers connect that with a more concentrated, more fragrant leaf. The soil is not strongly mineral the way Wuyi rock is, so Anxi tea is usually described through its cultivar and its making more than through its ground.

## What Anxi is known for {#known-for}

Anxi is known for oolong, and above all for Tieguanyin, which fills most of the county's tea land. Anxi is also where the trade places the craft of oolong making in southern Fujian, and where farmers learned to grow new plants from cuttings and layers instead of seed. Alongside Tieguanyin, four other local cultivars are well known: Huangjingui (also called Huangdan), Benshan, Maoxie and Meizhan. Each is sold as its own tea, and none of them is Tieguanyin. For the tea itself, how it is made and how it is sorted, see the Tieguanyin page.

## Xiping, Xianghua, Gande and the others {#townships}

Trade writing treats a few Anxi townships as names in their own right. Xiping, in the west, is where Tieguanyin is said to have begun, and its teas are usually described as the traditional kind, more oxidised and often roasted. Xianghua is high and often in cloud, and its tea is known for a strong, lasting taste. Gande is the newest of the three, lower, with younger bushes, and is tied to the lighter, greener style. Changkeng is often named as a fourth. Away from Tieguanyin, Daping is linked to Maoxie, Huqiu to Huangjingui and Lutian to Meizhan. A township is a pointer to the style of tea you may meet and is not a grade.

## Inner and outer Anxi {#inner-outer}

Sellers often speak of inner Anxi, the high mountain areas, and outer Anxi, the lower, warmer land nearer the county town. Inner Anxi is said to give the deeper, longer-lasting tea, and outer Anxi a quicker, brighter fragrance but a thinner cup. Places draw the line between the two in different ways, so treat it as a rough guide to height and not as a fixed border.

## A tea county with a long trading past {#history}

Anxi has grown tea for many centuries, and local sources trace it back to the late Tang. Tieguanyin itself is told as a story of the 1700s in Xiping, and the stories are folk accounts and not records. Oolong from southern Fujian was shipped out through Quanzhou and later Xiamen, and families from Anxi, many of them from Xiping, carried the tea and the trade to Southeast Asia, where they opened tea houses. Tea is still the main income of the county. Until the 1990s Anxi was one of the poorer counties in Fujian, and the Tieguanyin boom that followed changed that.

## Anxi on a label {#on-a-label}

Anxi Tieguanyin is a protected name, and Anxi on a label is a statement about where the leaf grew. It says nothing about the style, which can be light and green or roasted, and nothing about the grade. Not every tea sold as Tieguanyin comes from Anxi: some is grown elsewhere, and some is made from other cultivars under the name. A township on a label, such as Xiping or Xianghua, is a narrower claim than Anxi, and a label rarely proves it.
