## What sheng means {#identity}

Sheng is the undarkened branch of Pu’er, often called raw Pu’er in English. The label distinguishes it from shou, whose accelerated post-fermentation creates a markedly different tea from the outset. [cite:teaguardian-raw-cooked-puer]

## From maocha to finished tea {#processing}

Maocha names an intermediate state in tea production, while sheng describes the tea as it reaches the drinker. Sheng material may be sorted, dried, left loose, or steamed and compressed; the two terms overlap, but they describe the tea from different viewpoints. [cite:teaguardian-raw-cooked-puer]

## Change through storage {#aging}

Sheng is not given shou’s rapid fermentation. Its character instead changes gradually during storage, with leaf, liquor, aroma, and texture developing over years. Storage conditions therefore belong to the tea’s history, not as a guarantee of quality but as part of how any particular cake has evolved. [cite:marshaln-young-puer-2013]

## How raw pu’er is made {#making}

Raw pu’er begins as leaf from Yunnan’s large-leaf tea trees. It is picked and withered, then fried in a wok to kill the green. That heat stops most of the leaf’s own enzymes but not all, and because the leaf is never hot-air dried afterwards, the activity that survives is what lets the tea keep changing for years. It is rolled, then dried in the sun, turned over a couple of days. The result is maocha, the rough tea that is sold loose or pressed.

## Pressing {#pressing}

Maocha is pressed into cakes, bricks and tuo, small bowl shapes, either under a stone or by a hydraulic press; a stone-pressed cake is looser than a machine-pressed one. Pressing began so the tea could travel: compact cakes packed well onto pack animals on the old trade routes. It also decides how a tea ages, because a tight, large cake changes slowly and a loose or small one faster.

## How the trade grew {#history}

In the Qing dynasty pu’er went to the court as tribute, but far more was ordinary trade, carried as pressed tea along the Tea Horse Road to Tibet and Southeast Asia. In the twentieth century Hong Kong became its great drinking market, and merchants there kept it in humid stores to speed its change. After 1949 the private tea houses were folded into state factories, among them Menghai, Xiaguan and Kunming, which made pu’er to standard recipes under the state tea company’s labels.

## Collectors and mountains {#history-modern}

Through the 1980s and 1990s collectors, led at first by Taiwan, came looking for aged raw pu’er, and demand grew into a speculative boom in which fakes and falsely aged tea were common. Since that boom buyers have turned from factory brands to named mountains, villages and old trees, and prices for famous origins have risen.

## Storage {#storage}

Raw pu’er ages partly through slow oxidation and partly through the work of microbes, so heat and humidity decide how fast it changes. Humid, warm stores, the traditional storage of Hong Kong and Guangdong, change it quickly; dry, cool stores, with Kunming as the usual example, change it slowly and keep it brighter, and a store that is too dry can leave a tea stalled and green. The trade does not agree whether humid storage is a flaw or a style of its own. At home, keep a cake wrapped in paper, away from smells and direct sun, at roughly 60 to 75 percent humidity.

## Young raw pu’er {#age-young}

Under about three to five years a raw pu’er is young. Its cup is pale, yellow-green to pale gold, fresh and lean, with a bite of bitterness and astringency that is meant to turn sweet in the throat. Some drink it young for that liveliness; others treat it as a tea to put away.

## Raw pu’er with age {#ageing}

With years, a well-kept raw pu’er turns from pale gold toward amber and then red in the cup, and its bitterness and astringency soften as it grows smoother and sweeter. How fast depends on how it is pressed and stored. The trade does not agree where aged begins: some say ten years, many wait for twenty or more.

## Brewing raw pu’er {#brewing}

Young raw pu’er takes water a little off the boil, about 85 to 95°C, because hotter water draws out more of its bitterness; an aged one takes 95°C to boiling. A common start is about 7 grams to 100 ml and a quick rinse of a few seconds, then a first steep of ten to fifteen seconds, adding a few seconds each round. A good raw tea gives eight to ten cups or more.

## Breaking a pressed cake {#brewing-pressed}

Pry the leaf off a cake or brick in chunks with a pick or knife, from the surface and toward the centre, rather than crumbling it. A mix of larger pieces and smaller ones brews evenly.

## What people often notice {#common-impressions}

In young raw pu’er, a bitter, drying bite that turns into a returning sweetness, a feel in the throat that makes the mouth water, and fresh, floral or fruity notes such as apricot or plum. Tea kept in humid storage can carry a damp, earthy storage taste.

## What people often notice in aged raw pu’er {#notice-aged}

A smoother, thicker, sweeter cup with woody, herbal and camphor notes and something of dried dates or dark fruit.
