## What Liu An basket tea is {#definition}

Liu An basket tea is a dark tea packed into small bamboo baskets and kept for years. Despite its name it is made in Qimen County in southern Anhui, around Luxi and Rongkou, not in Lu'an, from the local small-leaf Qimen bush that Keemun red tea also comes from. In the south it was sold as Liu An, and sellers today use Liu An, Lu An, Lao Liu An and An tea for the same basket tea; the name is said to come from its following the Liu An way of making and trading. It is not Liu An Gua Pian, a green tea from the Lu'an area that only shares part of the name.

## How Liu An is made {#making}

Spring leaf, picked around Grain Rain, is first made much like a green tea: withered, fixed, rolled and dried into rough tea, which rests through the summer. In early autumn it is sorted, graded, blended and fired over charcoal. Then comes the step most accounts single out: the fired tea is spread on bamboo mats outdoors for one night to take the dew, and gathered before sunrise. It is steamed soft, packed into baskets lined with bamboo leaf, tied into bundles and fired a last time over charcoal, and it is sold only after resting for some years, commonly three.

## The basket and its tickets {#basket}

The tea is packed into woven bamboo baskets lined with bamboo leaf, from about 250 to 600 grams each, several tied into a bundle. The leaf lining gives the tea a bamboo note that sellers liken to rice dumplings, and drinkers keep the leaves to brew a small piece with the tea. Old baskets carry printed paper tickets naming the firm, which collectors use to tell editions and fakes apart. Sun Yi Shun is the best-known old firm, but modern baskets carry the name too, so the name alone does not date a basket.

## The southern trade {#southern-trade}

Basket tea went south to Guangdong, Hong Kong, Macau and the overseas Chinese of Southeast Asia, and that is where it was known and drunk. It was kept in homes and medicine shops, added to herbal remedies, and older drinkers in the south still call it a cooling tea in the traditional Chinese sense. Production stopped in the 1930s and 1940s, as war cut the southern trade, and the tea was nearly gone for decades. In 1983 tea people from Hong Kong sent an aged basket to Anhui and asked for it to be made again, and commercial making returned in the early 1990s.

## Old baskets {#old-stock}

Old baskets were kept in Hong Kong and Macau tea houses, medicine shops and homes, and the oldest are treated as collector's pieces, with prices that climb steeply with age. Fakes exist, so buyers look at the tickets, the basket's shape and the story of the stock.

## Keeping and ageing Liu An {#ageing}

Liu An is said to improve with years in its basket, turning mellower, rounder and redder in the cup. Young tea has an edge of charcoal or smoke that settles with age. Keep it dry, clean, away from smells and direct sun, in wrapping that breathes, not sealed airtight.

## Brewing Liu An {#brewing}

Use water at or near the boil, rinse the leaf quickly, and keep the steeps short, each a little longer than the last, in a gaiwan or clay pot; doses run from about 3 to 8 grams for 100 ml, for six to ten or more steeps. A small piece of the basket's bamboo leaf is often brewed with it. Aged tea can also be simmered in a larger pot, about 1 gram to 200 to 300 ml, for a few minutes or longer.

## What people often notice {#common-impressions}

Bamboo leaf above all, herbal or medicinal notes, a sweetness that returns after swallowing, a cooling feel in the mouth and throat, and a smooth, thick cup with little bitterness; young tea shows some smoke or charcoal, and the liquor runs from orange-gold to red.

## What people often notice in Liu An kept for years {#notice-aged}

A redder, browner liquor, the smoke gone, and the cup moving toward medicinal, woody and camphor notes, often with betel nut.
