## A Dali maker built on the Tibet trade {#maker-history}

Xiaguan Tea Factory is one of Yunnan’s long-running pu’er makers, based in the town of Xiaguan in Dali, at the foot of the Cang mountains beside Erhai lake. It began in 1941 as the Kangzang Tea Factory, set up to press tea for Tibet and the border regions, and the trade still knows it for pressed tea above all. Its signature is the tuocha, a small bowl-shaped pressed tea. The shape is older than the factory, since Xiaguan tea houses were already pressing it decades earlier, and it suited the trade because a dense pressed piece carried well on long caravan routes. People care about Xiaguan because it is one of the few big names in pu’er from outside Xishuangbanna, so it stands for a different tradition from Menghai.

## From state factory to company {#state-era}

After 1949 the factory came under the state tea company and, in the early 1950s, took the Zhong Cha mark that state factories used. In the mid 1950s the old private tea houses of Xiaguan were folded into it, and it settled into the name Yunnan Xiaguan Tea Factory. In the 1990s it was reorganised as a joint-stock company, and later it passed into private ownership. The year of that last step is given differently by different places and is not settled here. The effect for a buyer is that an older Xiaguan piece comes from the state factory era, while a newer one comes from a company, and the two should not be read as the same maker with the same record.

## Why the cup is called smoky {#house-style}

Xiaguan’s raw pu’er has a name for a smoky edge. Sellers and drinkers most often mention it first, and a trade saying holds that Xiaguan without smoke is not Xiaguan. Its best known cakes are the iron cakes, pressed hard under an iron press into a dense disc with flat edges, and dense compression is part of why young Xiaguan is slow to open and can taste sharp or bitter. Many sellers say the smoke softens and the tea turns rounder and sweeter with years in storage, which is why older Xiaguan raw cakes are sought out. How much smoke a given cake shows, and how fast it fades, varies by year and by storage, and no agreed cause for the smoke is established.

## Ripe pu’er from the 1970s {#ripe-pu-er}

Xiaguan is known for raw pressed tea, but it also makes ripe pu’er. Wet piling reached the factory in the 1970s, the same years Kunming and Menghai took it up, and a ripe fermenting workshop was running by the middle of that decade. Accounts differ on the exact year, so only the decade is firm. A ripe Xiaguan from the 1990s therefore comes from a factory that had been making ripe tea for some years, and it is reasonable to expect a bolder, earthier ripe cup than the soft, sweet Menghai style, though that comparison rests on a few sellers only.

## What the Xiaguan name tells you {#reading-the-name}

Xiaguan is a town and a maker. The name on a wrapper says where the tea was pressed and by whom. It does not by itself say where the leaf grew, how old the trees were, or what the recipe is. The factory has pressed raw and ripe tea, tuocha, bricks and cakes, under its own marks and under Zhong Cha, so a line such as a “wild” cake or an aged ripe brick has to be read from its own record. How the Xiaguan “wild” line is defined is not established: sellers use the words wild leaf or ancient wild tree on Xiaguan cakes, but no source read says what leaf stands behind the word. The place where Xiaguan sits, Dali, is covered on its own page.
