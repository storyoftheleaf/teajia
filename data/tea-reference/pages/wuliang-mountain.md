## Where Wuliang Mountain is {#where}

Wuliang Mountain is a long mountain range in west-central Yunnan, not a single village or peak. It starts near Nanjian in Dali Prefecture, runs south through Jingdong, Zhenyuan and Jinggu in Pu’er City, and fades out toward Ning’er and the old tea mountains of Xishuangbanna. The Lancang River (the upper Mekong) lies on its west side, and the range runs beside the Ailao Mountains. Its highest peaks are a little over 3,000 metres, though places give different names and figures for the top.

## A wet, forested mountain {#land-and-climate}

The range has a monsoon climate: a warm, rainy summer and a drier, misty winter, with large swings between day and night. Climate changes a lot from the valley floors to the high ridges, so the tea on one part of the range can differ from another. Much of it is still forest, and part is a national nature reserve. Old tea trees sit in that forest and in small gardens near villages.

## Old trees, wild and planted {#trees}

Wuliang Mountain is known for old tea trees, both wild ones in the forest and older planted ones in village gardens. Jingdong and Zhenyuan are the counties most often named for them. Tree age, leaf variety and garden height are quoted differently from seller to seller, so none of them is settled for the range as a whole.

## What the name means {#name}

Wuliang means “without measure.” The trade explains it as a name for a mountain too tall and wide to measure. When the name began to appear on tea wrappers is not established.

## Wuliang on a label {#on-a-label}

Wuliang Mountain on a label points to a whole range, and a label rarely says which county or village the leaf came from. Kunlu Mountain in Ning’er is a spur of the same range and has its own page. Qianjiazhai in Zhenyuan, home of a famous wild tea tree, is most often placed in the Ailao Mountains, not in Wuliang, though Zhenyuan sits between the two ranges and the line is not always drawn the same way. The trade says much tea sold as Wuliang comes from the northern part, near Jingdong and Nanjian.

## What people often notice {#common-impressions}

Sellers and tea writers most often describe a fragrant tea, with floral notes such as orchid, and a soft, fairly gentle cup with little bitterness. Some describe a fuller, sweeter body, and some find the tea thin. Reports differ because the range is so large and the teas come from different parts of it.
