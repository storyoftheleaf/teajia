## Where Guangdong is {#where-it-is}

Guangdong is a large province on the south coast of China, built around the Pearl River Delta. Its capital is Guangzhou, the old Canton, and Hong Kong and Macao sit beside it. The climate is hot and humid, with a rainy season and a drier one. Tea is grown mostly in the hills of the east and the north, away from the delta.

## What Guangdong is known for {#known-for}

Guangdong does not grow tea on the scale of Yunnan, Fujian or Hunan, and the trade knows it for two teas rather than for volume. One is the oolong of Phoenix Mountain, called dancong, which is the tea most buyers mean when they say Guangdong. The other is Yingde black tea. Beyond these, the province matters for what happens to tea after it is made: where it is traded, stored and drunk.

## Phoenix Mountain and dancong {#phoenix-dancong}

The oolong of Phoenix Mountain, in Chaozhou in the east, is the province's best known tea. It is valued for its strong scent, floral or fruity depending on the bush, and for old trees that give each tea its own character. The mountain, its villages and its trees have their own page, so this page does not repeat them. On a label, Guangdong alone says very little, and the mountain or village name is what points to the tea.

## Yingde black tea {#yingde-black}

Yingde, north of Guangzhou, makes the province's black tea. It is a young style by Chinese standards: machine production began in the late 1950s, and much of it has been sold abroad. Writers describe it as sweet, with a cocoa or chocolate note, and a malty, slightly peppery finish. It is not a mountain tea in the way Phoenix oolong is, and it should not be read as a fact about the province's other teas.

## A trading province {#trade-hub}

Guangdong's weight in tea comes as much from its ports and markets as from its gardens. Guangzhou has been a gateway for tea leaving China, and its Fangcun market is described as the largest tea trading centre in the south, with thousands of shops and merchants from many provinces, Hong Kong and Taiwan. Pu'er in particular moves through Guangzhou, and for most of the last century its main buyers were in Hong Kong and Taiwan. A tea sold in Guangzhou is therefore not a tea grown in Guangdong.

## How Guangdong drinks tea {#drinking-culture}

Tea is a daily habit here, in two very different forms. In the east, Chaozhou gongfu tea is the local way of brewing: small pots and cups, quick infusions, and a tea station at the front of many shops and homes. In Guangzhou and the delta, tea is drunk at yum cha, the morning or midday meal of a pot of tea with small dishes of dim sum. Pu'er, chrysanthemum and other teas are served this way, and pu'er has long been an everyday tea for ordinary people across the province.

## Hong Kong and Guangdong storage {#storage}

Hong Kong and Guangzhou are the places the trade names when it talks about traditionally stored pu'er. Both are hot and humid, which ages tea faster and darker than a dry climate does. Old pu'er sat for decades in the storerooms of Hong Kong teahouses and restaurants, and the "Hong Kong storage" label grew out of that. Whether a given tea sold under that name was really kept in Hong Kong, and how damp it was, is not something the label settles. See the pu'er pages for what storage does to the tea.
