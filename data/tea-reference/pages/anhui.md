## Where Anhui is {#where-it-is}

Anhui is a province in eastern China, inland from the coast and crossed by the Yangtze. Tea is grown only in its south and west, in the hills and mountains: the Huangshan range and the country around it in the south, and the Dabie Mountains in the west. The flat north of the province grows none. Most Anhui tea sits in a few separate pockets, each with its own famous tea, so the name Anhui on a label says less than the name of the county or mountain does.

## Why the hills suit tea {#land-and-climate}

The tea hills have a humid, subtropical climate with four clear seasons, plenty of rain, and mist and cloud that lie on the mountains for much of the year. People in the trade link that cover, together with acidic, well-drained mountain soil, to slow-growing leaf with a clean fragrance and a sweet aftertaste. The cold winters let the bushes rest, so spring leaf comes on strong. The west is cooler and drier than the south, which is why its tea grows on hardier bushes.

## What Anhui is known for {#known-for}

Anhui is one of the great tea provinces of China, and it is best known for green tea. Three green teas carry most of its name: Huangshan Maofeng from the Huangshan area, Taiping Houkui from the Taiping area, and Lu'an Guapian from the Lu'an area in the west. Its one world-famous red tea is Keemun, from Qimen County. Several of these are on the lists of China's famous teas and the trade often names Anhui among the provinces with many famous teas. Besides these, Anhui makes smaller teas such as Huoshan Huangya, a rare yellow tea, and Liu An basket tea, a dark tea.

## The places inside Anhui {#places-inside}

Four areas hold most of the fame. Huangshan, in the south, gives Huangshan Maofeng, a green tea with fine down on slightly curled leaf. Taiping, now part of the Huangshan area, gives Taiping Houkui, a long, flat green tea. Qimen, in the south-west, gives Keemun. Lu'an, in the west, gives Lu'an Guapian, a green tea made from single leaves with no bud and no stem, which is why it looks like a melon seed. Each of these is a separate tea with its own method, and none is a grade of another.

## Keemun and the red tea of Qimen {#keemun}

Keemun began in Qimen County in the late nineteenth century, when red tea making was brought into Anhui, which until then was known for green tea. It was sold abroad early and became a base for English blends. The trade describes it as a light, smooth red tea with a floral scent and little bitterness. Keemun in this sense is the tea of Qimen County and the hills around it, and the name does not cover other teas that are called Keemun style.

## Lu'an, Liu An and Lu'an Guapian {#lu-an-names}

The names Lu'an and Liu An look alike and are easy to mix up. Lu'an Guapian is a green tea from the city of Lu'an in western Anhui. Liu An basket tea is a dark tea made in Qimen County, in the south. It takes its name from the old Liu An way of making and trading, not from the city, and it is not green tea. A label that says Liu An or Lu'an needs the rest of the name to tell which tea is meant.
