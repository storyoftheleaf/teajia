# Oolong, a tea with many shapes

*Oolong sits between green and red, and the range inside it is wider than any other family.*

Oolong is partly oxidized tea. After picking, the leaves are withered, then shaken or tumbled so the edges bruise slightly, and left to change before being heated to stop the process. The amount of oxidation can be light or heavy, and the maker may also roast the tea afterward. That is why one table can pour two oolongs that seem to come from different worlds.

At one end are the lighter, rolled teas from mountain regions, often green in color, with a smell of orchid, cream and fresh flowers. At the other are the darker twisted or strip-shaped leaves from rocky hills, with a smell of roasted nuts, caramel, stone fruit and warm minerals.

Start with the dry leaf. Notice whether it is tightly rolled into small balls or long and twisted, whether it is green, brown or nearly black, and whether the smell is floral, fruity or toasty. Then smell it again after the leaf has been warmed in the pot. Often the whole character changes in that moment, deeper and louder than the dry leaf suggested.

Oolong is made to be poured many times. Rolled leaves open slowly, unfurling infusion by infusion until they fill the pot. The first cups can be shy. The middle ones are often the best. Watch how the aroma shifts, and how the texture of the tea in your mouth changes from light to thick, or from sharp to smooth.

The leaves tell a story too. When the pot is finished, look at the opened leaves. A whole leaf with a reddish rim shows where the bruising was.

**One small thing to try.** Choose one cup in the middle of the session and describe it in a single word, only for yourself. Do the same with the next cup. Words like sweet, thick, cooling, creamy or woody are enough. By the end you will have a small map of how the tea moved.
