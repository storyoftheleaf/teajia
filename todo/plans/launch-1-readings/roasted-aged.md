# Roasted and aged tea, warmth that takes time

*Some tea is finished by fire, some by years, and both ways change what is in the cup.*

Roasting is a craft of its own. After a tea is made, a maker can heat it slowly over charcoal or in an oven, often several times over many days, to dry it further and deepen its character. A light roast leaves the tea fresh. A heavy roast brings out toast, cocoa, burnt sugar and dark fruit. It is a way of choosing the voice of the tea after the leaf is already finished.

Aging is the same patience taken further. A tea kept dry for years, whether oolong, white, red or puer, loses its sharp edges. The smell moves from bright to settled, with notes of dried fruit, camphor, honey or old wood. Some aged teas are even roasted again every few years to keep them healthy and clean.

The words roasted and aged do not describe one family. They describe two things that can be done to many. At a table where such tea is poured, you are likely to meet a rock oolong with a mineral, charcoal warmth, or an older tea whose first sip feels remarkably round.

Start with the dry leaf, and notice how dark it is and whether it smells of fire, sugar or something quieter. Then warm the leaf in the pot, and pay attention to the moment the smell shifts. Roast often shows itself first, and the tea underneath comes forward later.

Be patient with the first cup. Roasted tea can taste a little closed at the start and open by the third. Aged tea can taste almost simple, then slowly reveal layers. Both reward a slow session more than a quick one.

**One small thing to try.** Rest the empty, warm cup near your nose after the second infusion, and close your eyes for a moment. The scent that lingers in the cup is the tea's quietest note, and you will probably find it is different from the one in the liquid.
