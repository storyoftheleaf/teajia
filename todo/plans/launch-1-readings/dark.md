# Dark tea, made slowly with time and microbes

*Puer and heicha are teas changed by fermenting, by ageing, or by both, and they ask you to slow down.*

Dark tea is a family of its own. After picking, the leaf is processed into a rough tea, then it goes through a long process in which microbes and moisture slowly change it. In some teas this happens in a pile over weeks. In others it happens in storage over years. Puer from Yunnan is the best known, and heicha, which means dark tea, covers others from Hunan, Guangxi, Sichuan and elsewhere.

Much dark tea is pressed into cakes, bricks or small nests, so you will see it as a solid piece to be pried apart. Do not worry about the breaking. Take a few leaves loose with a tea needle or knife, and notice how the pieces come away.

Smell the dry leaf. A young raw puer can smell of orchid, hay and green fruit. An older or ripe one often smells of wet earth, wood, camphor, dates and old books. Neither smell is a flaw, and for many people the smell of the cave, the cellar and the forest floor is exactly what they love.

Most dark tea begins with a quick rinse. Pour hot water over the leaf and pour it off right away. This wakes the leaf and clears the dust, and the smell of the wet leaf after the rinse is one of the best parts of the whole session.

The cup itself is often thick and smooth, from amber to near black. It has little bitterness when it is brewed gently, and tends to leave a sweetness behind. Dark teas hold up to many infusions, and their character changes slowly, not suddenly.

**One small thing to try.** After the rinse, lift the lid of the pot and smell the wet leaf. Say to yourself what the smell reminds you of. A place, a room, a season. It is often more accurate than any tasting word.
