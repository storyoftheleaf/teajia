# White tea, and the art of leaving it alone

*White tea is the quietest of the families, and it rewards a guest who arrives paying attention.*

White tea is made with very little. The leaves, often with their downy buds, are picked and then left to wither slowly, indoors or in gentle air, until they are dry. They are not rolled, not pan-fired, not shaped. Much of what you taste comes from the plant itself and from the patience of the withering.

Before the first pour, smell the dry leaf. It is often soft and low: hay, dried flowers, a little sweetness like warm straw. Some white teas look silvery and furred, others are darker, broader leaves that look almost like a pressed herb. Both are white tea, and they taste quite different.

The water for white tea is usually a little cooler than for other families, and the first infusion can be pale, almost clear gold. Do not mistake pale for weak. Lean in and notice the texture first: a light, slightly silky feel in the mouth, and a sweetness that arrives late rather than at once.

Across several infusions, white tea tends to move slowly. The first cup may be thin and fragrant, the third rounder, the fifth deeper in color and fuller in the body. Nothing dramatic happens, which is the point. It is a tea for noticing small changes.

Some white tea is also kept for years, and its character shifts toward something darker and more honeyed. If a young one and an older one are poured side by side at the table, that comparison alone is worth the evening.

**One small thing to try.** When the first cup is poured, hold off for ten seconds before you drink. Smell the cup, look at the color, and then take a first sip smaller than you normally would. Ask yourself what you noticed before the flavor. Then see if it is still there in the next cup.
