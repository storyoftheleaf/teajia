# Green tea, fresh from the pan

*Green tea is spring held in place, and a good one tells you exactly how it was handled.*

Green tea is made by stopping the leaf from changing. Soon after picking, the leaves are heated, most often in a hot pan or by steam, which halts the natural browning. Then they are shaped and dried. Because nothing is allowed to oxidize, the tea stays close to the taste of fresh leaf.

Smell the dry leaf first. You may find toasted chestnut, fresh-cut grass, seaweed, orchid or sweet beans. The pan-fired teas of China often smell nutty and warm, while steamed teas, common in Japan, lean toward green, marine and vegetal. Neither is better. They simply come from different hands and different habits.

Look at the leaf. Some are flat and pressed, some twisted into small curls, some needle-fine. The shape came from the maker and often hints at the cup.

Water matters here. Green tea prefers it hot but not boiling, and a short steep. It can turn bitter quickly if rushed, and that bitterness is only a lesson in timing, not a failure. A good green tea has a clear, bright color, a sweetness that follows any brisk edge, and a clean finish that stays in the mouth.

Over several infusions, notice how the first cup is often the most aromatic and the later ones become softer and rounder. Some teas open slowly and surprise you at the third pour.

Green tea does not keep as long as other families, and freshness is part of what you are tasting. A tea from this spring and the same kind from three years ago are, in a way, different drinks.

**One small thing to try.** After you drink the first cup, smell the empty cup. The warmed porcelain or clay holds the aroma differently from the liquid, often sweeter and deeper. Compare it with the dry leaf you smelled earlier, and see which one you would describe in different words.
