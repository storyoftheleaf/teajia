# Red tea, the full turn of the leaf

*What English speakers call black tea is called red in Chinese, for the color of the cup.*

Red tea is fully oxidized. After picking, the leaves are withered and then rolled or pressed to break their cells, and they are left to darken in a warm, humid place. Oxygen does the work, turning green leaf to brown and copper, and changing the flavor from fresh and grassy to sweet, malty and deep. Then the leaf is dried to hold it there.

The name matters because it changes what you look for. In a gongfu cup, red tea is not the strong, dark drink that is steeped for minutes and softened with milk. It is clear and amber, often the color of dark honey or ripe plum, and it has more to say than people expect.

Begin by smelling the dry leaf. It might be sweet potato, cocoa, dried fruit, honey, malt, or a light smoke. Some red teas are made of long, wiry black leaves, some with golden tips that look like fine hair. Warm the leaf and smell again, and you may notice a ripe, warm sweetness that was hiding.

In the mouth, look for body. Red tea tends to feel rounder and warmer than green or white, and a good one has sweetness without any sugar. It is also forgiving, and often gives a good many infusions before it fades.

Notice how the color holds. A well-made red tea stays bright and clear. If it clouds as it cools, that is a small, normal thing, and you can simply note it.

Different regions make very different red teas. Some are malty, some are floral, some taste of dried longan or cinnamon. If two are poured together, notice what is shared and what is not.

**One small thing to try.** Pour a small amount of the tea into a white cup or bowl if one is offered, and hold it against the light. Look at the color from the edge to the center, and name it as you would name a paint: honey, amber, rust, garnet.
